Week 3 · Wednesday — Voices

Act I · Hearkening to Voices · whose voice do you carry that you don't hear anymore?
Only class this week — Monday, Sept 7 is Labor Day. Voice Print launches, drafts, and heads home to compose today.
Today's goals: Hear how one remembered voice brings a person alive, learn what makes dialog feel real, and launch Voice Print — a moment with a voice you don't hear anymore, in pure dialog.

0 · Daily Poem — "Words" (~10 min)
  • Missing Persons, "Words" (1982). Read the lyric on the page first, then hear it sung. The whole song is one voice asking whether anybody on the other end is actually listening. What does the singing do to those words that the page alone doesn't — and what happens to a question when it's repeated that many times?

Purpose: the standing ritual — the week's move from the other side, a voice straining to be heard rather than one strained after.

1 · Housekeeping + Writing Place debrief (~5 min)
  • One thing you learned photographing your writing place.

2 · Listen — "My Father's Voice" + Daspit (~20 min)
  • Romano, pens down (~4 min): listen to "My Father's Voice" — WMUB 88.5, Father's Day 2006, three and a half minutes. Write down every word you hear him put in quotation marks.
  • The revision (~6 min): here is the radio, at the moment his father calls him in from the game —
    Then came my father's voice, short, booming, unmistakable, calling my name.
    and here is the same sentence in WWM ch9, written years later —
    The first call came: my father's voice, short, booming, unmistakable. "Tom!"
    One word added — and the piece is now built on it. On the radio he describes the voice; on the page he lets it speak. That is this week's move, and Romano made it to his own draft. (His notebook was different again: "Tom! Come on home!" — four words, cut to one.) One passage crossed over almost untouched, his uncle Gigi Chiavari answering why he came to America at eighteen —
    "Why I come 'cross?" Uncle Gigi said. "Same reason everybody come 'cross. You make a better living, a better home, a better life."
    Ask why this is the one thing that never needed changing. He had carried it in his notebook twenty-five years.
  • Daspit, in pairs (~6 min): "'None of Us'" — four poems, 2017 to 2020. The last one ends on the exchange the title comes from, in post-intensive care:
    "if I lost all of this" and he waves his IV and monitor laden arms at the various tubes he's connected to "would I just disappear?" "no dad, you won't disappear none of us in this hospital will. none of us."
    The action sits inside the question, between its halves — the pause is real. Neither says a word about love or dying; it is a question about tubes. Now go find more. Bring back two: the line that surprised you, and one you don't think is Dad speaking at all.
  • Whole group (~4 min): read your finds aloud. Romano's claim, flatly — "We remember the voice, and the person comes alive." Voice Print asks for one moment in pure dialog. What did Romano gain by cutting his four words to one — and what would Daspit lose if you did that to his ending?

Purpose: the author revising description into quotation, in his own two versions, is the lesson — they don't have to take it on trust. Back pocket — the radio carries what the chapter drops (his father dead at 59, hit head-on by drag racers; Romano was 15) and the chapter carries what the radio drops (lightning bugs, Whitey Ford, "Tom!"). In Daspit: 12.10.17, where "the forest" and "beyond" read as dementia until Mom explains they are clubs on the old highway. Not-Dad lines: Mom's "Dad never liked Hank Williams much." and the cafeteria worker's "back honey?"

3 · What makes dialog real (ch10) (~20 min)
  • The model (~4 min): ch10 prints a coffee-shop exchange Romano had with a math education colleague. Pure dialog — not one tag, not one line of description, and you never lose track of who is speaking.
    "Fun? That's easy: a rich mathematical problem!" "Fun for English teachers and their students." "Still easy. I've seen you teach. You love to have fun." "I do. And I want students to enjoy my classes, but I've got some cognitive dissonance." "Yeah?" "Learning to be literate is serious business. I'm uneasy with fun." "Ah! You want fun, but you want deep fun." "Yes! Deep fun! Like deep massage."
    This is exactly what Voice Print asks you for. Note how short the turns get as they close in.
  • Real talk (~5 min): Silver Linings Playbook — two people compare medications at a dinner party.
    "You ever take Klonopin?" "Klonopin, yeah." "Right?" "Jesus." "What day is it?"
    Five turns, eleven words, and the joke lands without anyone explaining it. Then watch the table turn: "I'm tired. I want to go." — "No, no, no, we haven't even finished the salad yet. Or the duck." — "I said I'm tired. Are you going to walk me home or what?" — "You mean me?" Nobody finishes a thought; the fight happens in the fragments.
  • The exception (~5 min): Birdman opens as an exchange — "It's my chance to finally do work that means something." / "Means something to who?" — and then stops being one. His daughter talks for a solid minute and he never gets a turn back, down to "You're doing this because you're scared to death, like the rest of us, that you don't matter. And you know what? You're right. You don't." Romano says people don't deliver speeches. She delivers one. Why does it work here — and what did it cost her to be the only one talking?
  • Romano's rules, checkable (~5 min): record exact words, don't tell "the gist"; simple tags, used only when needed; no adverbs propping them up (he asked questioningly); new paragraph per speaker; brief exchanges — "people usually talk to each other; they don't deliver speeches"; say it aloud. He prints an imagined conversation with his dying friend Ken Brewer and hands you the tally — 21 turns, 8 tags, 7 of them "says" — go count him. And the ten-second test: "Ah, I love the smell of the ocean. It's so strong you can taste it," he said as he sucked the air deep within his lungs. Try speaking and inhaling at once. Romano: "I'll call the life squad."

Purpose: a pure-dialog model, a scene that works by fragments, and a scene that breaks the rule on purpose — then the rules, verifiable by counting. Note: the two film passages are transcribed from auto-captions; check them against the clips before printing.

4 · Launch Voice Print + gush the voice (~25 min)
  • Meet Voice Print: one moment with a voice you don't hear anymore, in pure dialog.
  • In Journaler, 5-minute timer, gush the exchange — just the two voices, back and forth, the words only they used. Real or imagined. Gush mode locks editing.

Purpose: get the voices on the page while the sound of them is close.

Wrap-up & at home:
  • Compose Voice Print from your gush — one page, pure dialog, simple tags, new paragraph per speaker. Read it aloud to test it. (Due Fri · Canvas.)
  • Read J. Edwards, "Beyond Presence" (the Invisibility scene) + WWM ch11 · ch12 · ch13 (through "Make Me Taste It") for Monday.
  • Also read Chiang, "ChatGPT Is a Blurry JPEG of the Web". His claim: a language model hands back a lossy copy of what it read — close enough to look right, with the detail thrown away. Hold that against the sensory detail we practise all week, and keep it: in Week 5 we press your own writing through the machine ten times and watch what survives.
