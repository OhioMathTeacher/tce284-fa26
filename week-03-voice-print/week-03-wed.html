Forty-two years now since his death, when I was fifteen. Forty-two years since I have heard his voice. It has been many years since I've heard my father's voice. Every other number in the piece survives — 1914, fifty-four, 1940, 1936, ninety-three. Why not this one?
After you've argued it out — where the "Tom!" came from, and why the rest went

It isn't new. Ch9 prints the notebook entry the whole essay grew from, written years before the broadcast during fifteen minutes of rambling with a group of Georgia teachers:

I can think back 45 years ago, before my father died, and I imagine a long day ending in midsummer, the sun long set, but still just a little light yet to make shadows and I hear my father call out from the top of the steps, "Tom! Come on home!"

Four words in the notebook. None on the radio. One in the book. He had the quotation from the start, gave it up when he read the piece aloud, and took it back — cut to a single syllable — when it went to the page.

Why the quotation only works on the page. On the radio his own voice is already in the room, so speaking his father's words means performing him. On the page there is no sound at all, and printing the word is the only way to make it happen. The chapter also has a job the commentary doesn't: it argues that "We remember the voice, and the person comes alive" — so it had better let the man speak.

Why the numbers go. The doubled "Forty-two years … Forty-two years" is an ear device; a listener gets one pass and can't reread. And it is the one count measured from now, in a book still being assigned a decade later.

Purpose: hear it as listeners first, then let them find what changed on the way to the page.

Open after they've argued it — who is actually talking

Not Dad: the Hank Williams line is Mom. In the last poem, "good luck." is strangers on the elevator, "don't panic…" is his sister on the phone, "back honey?" is the woman scooping taco salad in the cafeteria — and the closing three lines are Toby himself.

Which means the title comes from the one line his father doesn't say. In four poems about Dad, the words on the cover are the son's.

The count is the argument. One line, four, one, nine. The thin column is 6-19-2020 — the poem after the death, where the only voice left is Mom's. Then the last poem goes back in time, to the six weeks after the stroke, and gives him nine. Daspit ends the sequence with his father alive, mid-question, a month out.

Watch the second column, too. "the forest" and "beyond" read as dementia until Mom explains they were clubs on the highway between where she grew up and where he did. The quotation marks are doing the work: Daspit records the words exactly, withholds the gloss, and lets you make the same mistake the waitress made.

Purpose: a man built almost entirely out of quoted lines — and a table that shows how few it takes.

3 · What makes dialog real (ch10) (~20 min)
  • Watch two exchanges: Silver Linings Playbook and Birdman. What makes the talk feel real — the fragments, the overlaps, what is not said?
  • A conversation of Romano's own, from Chapter 10 — he ran into a colleague, a math education teacher, at a coffee shop early one morning. Neither speaker is named:
    "Fun? That's easy: a rich mathematical problem!" "Fun for English teachers and their students." "Still easy. I've seen you teach. You love to have fun." "I do. And I want students to enjoy my classes, but I've got some cognitive dissonance." "Yeah?" "Learning to be literate is serious business. I'm uneasy with fun." "Ah! You want fun, but you want deep fun." "Yes! Deep fun! Like deep massage."
    Which one is Romano and which is the math teacher? How can you tell? How do you know when each one starts speaking? And how many dialog tags do you count?
  • Romano's rules for writing dialog, from Chapter 10: exact words, not the gist; simple tags, mostly "said"; no adverbs propping them up; new paragraph per speaker; short exchanges; say it aloud. Do the two clips follow them?

4 · Launch Voice Print + gush the voice (~25 min)
  • Meet OP3 · Voice Print: one moment with a voice you don't hear anymore, in pure dialog.
  • In Journaler, 5-minute timer, gush the exchange — just the two voices, back and forth, the words only they used. Real or imagined. Gush mode locks editing.
  • After the timer, Journaler's reflection partner is optional. If you open it, it asks how the writing went — never what the voice should say. It does not write the dialog for you.

Purpose: get the voices on the page while the sound of them is close.
