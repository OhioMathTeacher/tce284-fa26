"My Father's Voice" — transcript

Tom Romano, read on WMUB 88.5, Oxford, Ohio · Father's Day 2006 · 3:34
Transcribed from the 2006 recording. Timestamps are approximate.
0:00

I remember my father calling my name on summer evenings in the late 1950s when I was a boy in northeastern Ohio. As night fell, I played Wiffle ball with neighbor kids in the yard behind Doc Stires's office. We'd all promised to be home before dark, but no one was willing to break the spell. Our play was summer and friendship and a tad of rebellion, to be outside in the descending night beyond the time our parents said to be home.

0:30

Then came my father's voice, short, booming, unmistakable, calling my name.

0:36

My father's voice, no trace in it of an Italian accent. He had been in America since 1914, when he left Nola, a village near Naples, he then just a boy of nine.

0:47

My father's voice, still strong at fifty-four years old — though he would be dead at fifty-nine, the victim of two drag racers roaring down a public road, smashing head-on into his car.

0:56

My father's voice calling from behind the screen door of our apartment over his tavern and bowling alleys. He had owned those businesses since 1940, after years of working in brickyards that had drawn Italian immigrants to Malvern, Ohio.

1:15

My father's voice rising into the night sky, carrying over rooftops, alley, and neighbors' yards to land in my ears where I played with friends in the yard behind Doc Stires's office. I ran all the way home toward the warm yellow light emanating from our apartment.

1:35

My father had worked hard to be successful in America, and like the Italian accent he naturally left behind when he learned English, he also left behind his ethnicity.

1:48

There's a photograph in an oval frame of my father and two of his brothers taken after the family arrived in America — Giuseppe, Antonio, and Felice, my father — all dressed in soft caps with short bills, belted waistcoats, white shirts buttoned to their necks, knickers, and sturdy leather shoes laced over their ankles. Look, says the photo, a print of which was surely sent to relatives in Italy. Look how well we are doing in America.

2:19

I heard my father speak Italian only to immigrants who had come to America when they were adults and had never learned English with the ease and facility their children did. Otherwise, my father never spoke Italian. The old country was a distant memory that had little to do with the identity he had forged for himself. He was an American businessman. He valued decorum, disliked clannishness, discouraged talk in his tavern about politics and religion. Those led to loud arguments, which were bad for business.

2:51

I once asked an uncle of mine, Gigi Chiavari, my father's brother-in-law who lived to be ninety-three, why he had come to America.

3:03

“Why I come 'cross?” Uncle Gigi said. “Same reason everybody come 'cross. You make a better living, a better home, a better life.”

3:10

That was what my father had done. Forty-two years now since his death, when I was fifteen. Forty-two years since I have heard his voice. But writing this, I hear it now. My father's voice at twilight on a summer evening in small-town America. My father's voice calling me home.
