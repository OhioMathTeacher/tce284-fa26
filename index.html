About this course

TCE 284 is a writing course for future teachers. You will strengthen your own written voice, write across a range of genres, study how successful writers work, respond to each other's drafts, meet one-to-one in conferences, and revise. Everyone in the room is preparing to teach, so the stakes are real: you will model for a room of children what a writer is and does.

This semester the course writes under one condition. When a machine can generate fluent prose, images, audio, and video on demand, what is left that only you can do? We answer that question by making things. AI is not a unit in this course; it is the water we write in. In every act you will work alongside it, test it, and learn where it helps and where it hands you the generic version of what you meant to say. The writer's job is the specific, the particular, and the true, which is the one thing an averaging machine cannot manufacture for you.

The arc of the semester. Three acts, each deepening a single writing gesture, and in each one AI plays a different role.

  • Act I — Become a Writer (Wks 1–5). Five short One-Pagers, each a tiny scene from your own life plus a reflection on what it means now. You draft each one in Journaler under a timer, so the writing is yours by construction. Act I closes with the Generation Loss lab: you press one of your own pieces through an AI over and over and watch it decay toward the generic. Here AI is a foil.
  • Act II — The Currere (Wks 6–10). One thread from your life, traced across past, present, and an imagined future, then woven into a single piece. You will storyboard it, render its key scenes, meet twice in one-to-one conferences, and revise. Here AI is a studio tool: it can help you explore and question, but it never writes your words.
  • Act III — Multimodal Research Project (Wks 11–15). A research project on a topic you care about, built from real sources (including one you gather yourself) and rendered across many forms: writing, image, audio, video. Here AI is a research partner, grounded in your own sources.

Running underneath all three: the look-back spine. In Week 1 you write to the question why do we write? In the last week you write a letter back to that Week 1 writer. Reflection on writing you actually did is the part of this course a machine cannot fake for you.

Course objectives

By the end of the course you will be able to:

  • Draft with less self-censoring and revise with more purpose, using the habits of practicing writers.
  • Write in several genres and adapt voice, form, and structure to your subject and audience.
  • Read published writing as a writer, naming the craft moves that make it work.
  • Give and use honest peer response, and revise across drafts and conferences.
  • Compose across modes (writing, image, audio, video) and choose the form a piece needs.
  • Use AI deliberately and disclose it honestly, judging where it serves your writing and where it flattens it.
  • Reflect on your own writing in a way that no tool can do for you.

The Writer's Notebook

Your notebook is where the everyday writing of this course lives. You write in it in nearly every class and on your own, and you turn the whole thing in on Wednesday, December 2 — our last class meeting. You will write the Look-Back Letter in that session, so the notebook is complete on the day you hand it over.

It is graded on kept practice, not polish: the point is that you wrote, often, without stopping to fix or judge. No one red-pens a free-write. Aim for at least one entry per class meeting — expect 20 or more entries by December.

Into the notebook go: every free-write, including the Week 1 why do we write? baseline and the daily openers; in-class quick-writes and exercises; your currere gushes, brainstorms, and storyboard notes; your research topic map and source notes; what you make of the readings; and the Week 15 letter back to your Week 1 self.

Marking a passage in Journaler is note-taking: it keeps the passage and cites its page, but on its own it is not an entry. It becomes one when you write what you make of it — My Progress in Journaler lists every reading still waiting on you.

Your timed One-Pager gushes do not go here. Each one is submitted with the One-Pager it produced, on the second sheet of that PDF, together with the record of the writing session. It is the same writing either way, and it belongs with the piece it became.

The polished projects are turned in separately — the notebook is the raw material behind them. You keep it in Journaler, which dates and numbers your entries, builds the Contents list, and turns the whole thing in with one click. If a computer is a real barrier for you, come talk to me.

This is also where attendance lives. There is no separate attendance grade in this course, because an entry per class session already ties your grade to showing up and writing. See Attendance under Policies for what being here does and does not affect.

Working with AI

AI is part of this course, and using it well is one of the things you are here to learn. That is different from letting it do your writing. The policy follows the three acts.

  • Act I (One-Pagers). You draft in Journaler under a timer that locks editing while it runs. That timed, in-app session is the proof the writing is yours. Do not paste in outside or generated text. After the timer, an AI reflection partner may ask you about the experience of writing, never about the words themselves.
  • Act II (Currere). AI is a studio partner for thinking and questioning. Your currere is hand-written. AI does not draft your scenes or your close.
  • Act III (Research Project). You will use AI as a grounded research partner and you must transform what it produces. Raw, unedited AI output does not count as your work, and there is a cap on how much raw output a project may include. A short reflective note on how you used AI is part of the grade.

Disclosure is required everywhere. Every piece you turn in carries a short note on how you used AI — what tool, for what, at which step. Honest disclosure is never penalized. Undisclosed or prohibited use is an integrity violation.

Attendance

There is no attendance grade and I do not take roll. Attendance is already inside the assignments. Your Writer's Notebook is graded on kept practice at roughly an entry per class session, your five One-Pagers are drafted in class under a timer, and most days are three to five short activities rather than a lecture. The notebook is the record, and you keep it, not me.

If you miss a session, the day's outline is on this site. Do the writing and it counts, exactly as if you had been here. Journaler runs anywhere, and the timer that keeps a One-Pager draft honest works at your kitchen table as well as in 222 McGuffey.

What attendance actually affects, concretely:

  • The two conferences, 10 points. Conference Packet in Week 7 and Draft Conference in Week 9 are one-to-one meetings about your currere. There is no version of these you can do alone. If you cannot make your slot, tell me and we will reschedule, but they have to happen.
  • Peer response and read-aloud revision, Weeks 9 and 13. Your draft gets read by a person who is not you. That feedback shapes work that is graded, so missing it costs you indirectly even though the session carries no points of its own.
  • The Generation Loss lab in Week 4 and the share-out on the last day. Both are things the room does together, and neither reproduces from an outline.
  • Your Writer's Notebook, at roughly an entry per session. This one you can make up from that day's outline.

The pattern is simple: if it involves other people, being here is the assignment. If it is writing, the writing is the assignment.

If you know you will miss, message me beforehand. You do not owe me a reason. Nothing is deducted for an absence, so there is nothing to excuse and no proof to produce. If something serious is happening in your life, tell me and we will move your deadlines. If you go quiet for a stretch I will check in, not to scold you, but because people who disappear from a writing course are usually carrying something.

Late work

Anything may be up to 48 hours late. No penalty, no need to ask, and no explanation required. Submit it and move on.

If you need longer than that, email me. You will get it. I would rather read your real work a week late than a panicked version on time. There is nothing to prove and no documentation to produce.

One thing cannot slide, and for the same reason attendance matters: other people are waiting on it.

  • Drafts that go to a conference or a peer review. Someone else has to read it, so it has to exist. If yours will not be ready, tell me before the session and we will find another use for the time.

The end of term has a hard edge, and it is a calendar, not a rule of mine. The Multimodal Research Project is due Wednesday, December 9. The 48-hour grace still applies, which carries it to Friday, December 11 — but nothing goes past that. Grades are due to the university the following Tuesday and there is no room behind it.

There is nothing to keep track of here. No tokens to count, no percentage per day, no ledger.

Privacy

Everything on this public site is de-identified — no student names, work, or grades. Much of what you write this term is about your own life. You choose what a finished piece shows: redact, blur, or code what stays private. Withholding is part of the composition, and you are graded on craft and judgment, never on candor or how much you reveal.

Course tools

What you need

Any current browser works, including Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari, on macOS, Windows, Linux, or a Chromebook. There is nothing to install and no account to create.

We recommend writing on a laptop or desktop. You turn in both the One-Pagers and the writer's notebook by printing them to PDF from your browser, and phones and tablets handle printing inconsistently. A larger screen also makes the timed drafts easier.

Journaler also works on an iPad. We recommend testing Journaler early and getting in the habit of using it on the same device each week.

Your work is saved in the browser you used, on the computer you used. It does not follow you to another machine on its own. Click ⤓ Save my work at the end of every session and keep the file somewhere you own, such as a cloud drive or a thumb drive. That single file carries your writing and your readings together, and ⤒ Open my file restores it on any machine. If you work in a computer lab or on a school-managed machine, treat this as required rather than optional: those machines can erase browser data when you log out.
