Week 8 · Wednesday — Break a Rule, Share the Room

Act II · currere · Grammar B, on purpose
Have your six cards with you today. You'll pull one and write straight from it.
Today's goals: Fix a poem and find out what fixing costs, free-write twice — a broken rule and a thread that talks — share a little aloud, and say what currere is.

0 · Daily Poem — Fix it (~10 min)
  • Fix (3 min). With a partner, take the note on the handout. A famous poet wrote it to someone they love. Fix some things before they send it — look for one or more "errors": capitals, punctuation, grammar. Then we share what we found.
  • Reveal (3 min). E. E. Cummings, "[since feeling is first]" (1926). Then see what a machine does with it: Telefone in Journaler.
  • Speculate (4 min). Who pays any attention to the syntax of things?
  • More than we have time for: the poem, mark by mark →

Purpose: the syntax-breaker's own argument for breaking syntax — exactly the permission today's free-write runs on.

1 · Grammar B — you already own these (~10 min)
  • Quick refresher, not a re-teach. The four moves from Breaking the Rules: sentence fragments · labyrinthine sentence · purposeful misspelling · double voice. Glance back at the Grammar B examples if you need a jog.
  • Ask yourself: which of these might a moment in my currere want? Where would a broken rule tell it truer?

Purpose: bring the tools you already felt in Act I back into the room, aimed now at the currere.

2 · Two free-writes (~20 min)
  • Break a rule (10 min). Take one of your six moments — one card, in front of you. In Journaler, start the timer and free-write it, breaking at least one rule on purpose. Gush mode locks editing, so break freely — let a fragment land, let one sentence run on and on, bend a word so its spelling carries the meaning, run two voices at once. Every "error" is one you mean.
  • Let the thread talk (10 min). Cummings gave his blood, his brain, and Spring a voice. Do that to your thread. Take the thread running through your currere — longing, fear, hope, searching, wondering — and write as it. It has been with you the whole time. What does it have to say? Mine, in three drafts →
  • A keeper? Paste it into your Google Doc, under its card.

Purpose: two ways in that a clean draft can't reach — the moment with a rule broken, and the thread speaking for itself.

3 · Share it out (~15 min)
  • A few volunteers read from either free-write. For a broken rule, the room names which move it used — and whether it helps the writing or gets in the way. For a thread that talks: whose voice is that?

Purpose: hear the moves out loud in each other's work — a broken rule sounds different read aloud, and listeners can tell you whether it works.

4 · Currere check-in (~25 min)
  • How's it going? (5 min). How are you feeling about the currere process so far? Comments, concerns, questions — open floor.
  • Around the room (10 min). Each person offers one thing: a line of your own writing you're proud of, or an idea about writing that's gotten clearer. No commentary needed — just put it in the room.
  • What is currere, anyway? (10 min). Write the elevator speech: someone asks what you're writing, and you have the ride to the fourth floor to explain. A few read theirs. Your elevator speech could be useful to include in your First Draft — authors often explain currere in their writing and cite Pinar. Look back at J. Edwards, "Beyond Presence: The Importance of Being Seen" (Weeks 3–4), for an example.

Purpose: the last whole-group moment before you go solo on the draft — name what the currere has taught you before you assemble it.

Wrap-up & before Monday:
  • Assemble your six moments into your First Draft — one connected piece, not six separate chunks. Help the reader see how each scene leads to the next.
