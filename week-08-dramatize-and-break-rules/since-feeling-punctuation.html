Mark by Mark

E. E. Cummings, "[since feeling is first]" (1926) · what the punctuation is doing — and not doing

We only had ten minutes with this poem. Here's what we didn't get to. Every mark below was put there — or left out — on purpose.

since feeling is first
who pays any attention
to the syntax of things
will never wholly kiss you;

wholly to be a fool
while Spring is in the world

my blood approves,
and kisses are a better fate
than wisdom
lady i swear by all flowers. Don’t cry
—the best gesture of my brain is less than
your eyelids’ flutter which says

we are for each other: then
laugh, leaning back in my arms
for life’s not a paragraph

And death i think is no parenthesis

1 · The first stanza means three things at once

No punctuation after "things" — so read it three ways, and all three are true:

  • A statement. "Who" means whoever: whoever pays attention to the syntax of things will never wholly kiss you.
  • A question. Since feeling is first, who pays any attention to the syntax of things? Nobody. Obviously.
  • A fragment. The syntax of things will never wholly kiss you. Grammar can't love you back.

Add a period or a question mark and you have to pick one. Two meanings die. That is what "fixing" it costs.

2 · Lowercase "i," capital "Spring"

The speaker is small. The season is big. The poem's capitals rank what matters — and the poet comes second to spring.

3 · A period in the middle of a line

lady i swear by all flowers. Don’t cry

The sentence ends. The line doesn't. The feeling doesn't stop for the period. (And "swear by all flowers" is an oath — the way you'd swear to God — not a bouquet.)

4 · The dash

—the best gesture of my brain is less than

The brain enters as an interruption. Even its best gesture loses — to an eyelid.

5 · The colon

we are for each other: then / laugh, leaning back in my arms

A colon makes a promise: what comes next explains what came before. So what proves "we are for each other"? Not an argument. A laugh, and leaning back. The colon hands the proof to the body.

And look where the poem's only semicolon and only colon go — both to love, both to and then, and then. Living gets the marks that keep going.

6 · No paragraph, no parenthesis — and no period for death

for life’s not a paragraph // And death i think is no parenthesis

A parenthesis is an aside — the part you could skip. Death isn't that. And after a parenthesis the sentence picks back up; after death, it doesn't.

Now count. The poem has no parentheses anywhere. Its stanzas break mid-sentence, so it has no tidy paragraphs either. And the last line — the death line — gets no period. The poem says death is no parenthesis, and then refuses to close it with anything at all.

7 · What the machine did

Telefone "fixed" it and called the result 88% the same. It capitalized the "i." It turned the eyelids' wordless flutter into dialogue, in quotation marks. It swapped "wholly" — which sounds like holy — for "truly." And it put a period at the end. Death got its period after all.

So — who pays any attention to the syntax of things?

Cummings does, more than anyone. You can't break a rule on purpose without paying attention to it. That's the difference between a mistake and breaking the rules in style (Romano, ch15). And now, reading this — so do we.
