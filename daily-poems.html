Daily Poems

TCE 284 · Writing for Educators · Fall 2026

Most sessions open with a poem — read aloud, talked about for ten minutes, then left to do its work. Nothing to prepare and nothing to turn in. They are collected here with the question each one opens, so you can find one again afterwards, catch up on a session you missed, or read ahead if you want to.

Sixteen of them, Weeks 1–10. The ritual belongs to Acts I and II; Act III turns to research and drafting.

Act I — Become a Writer · Weeks 1–5

Paul Laurence Dunbar, “We Wear the Mask” Week 1 · Wed, Aug 26 · ~5 min

  • Read the poem — Dunbar wrote it in Dayton, Ohio. What do you notice? Who is the “we”? What does the mask cost, and what does it protect?

Purpose: start inside real language — and the mask is Romano's question too: who gets to speak, and what do we hide?

George Ella Lyon, “Where I'm From” Week 2 · Mon, Aug 31 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — what do you notice: the lists, the specific names, the foods and sayings? The poem is built to be borrowed. Where are you from?

Purpose: the standing ritual — and a place poem you can emulate, opening the Writing Place theme with your own roots.

Shel Silverstein, “Sick” Week 2 · Wed, Sep 2 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — Romano writes about place, risk and all, seeking surprise, and seeking pleasure in language. Which of those do you see on display in a classic children's poem?

Purpose: the standing ritual — the writer's place as a dwelling, on the day you build your own.

Missing Persons, “Words” (1982) Week 3 · Wed, Sep 9 · ~10 min

  • Read the lyric, then hear it sung. One voice asking whether anybody on the other end is listening. What does the singing do to those words that the page doesn't?

Purpose: the standing ritual — a voice straining to be heard, on the day we listen for one.

Galway Kinnell, “Blackberry Eating” Week 4 · Mon, Sep 14 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — which words make you taste it: “the black art of blackberry-making,” the lumpy words he loves to “squeeze, squinch open, and splurge”? Point to the exact ones.

Purpose: the standing ritual — and a poem that does the day's move (words you can taste), so the target is in the room before we name it.

Seamus Heaney, “Mid-Term Break” Week 4 · Wed, Sep 16 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — Heaney never says he's grieving; he shows it: the knelling bells, the baby cooing, the old men standing to shake his hand, and the last line. Where does the grief land, and how does he get it there without naming it?

Purpose: the standing ritual — and the day's move at its highest level: an emotion shown, never told.

Jim Daniels, “Short Order Cook” Week 5 · Mon, Sep 21 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — where does it move fast, break, and refuse to sit still? What rules is it not following, and why does it work anyway?

Purpose: the standing ritual — and a poem that already breaks rules in style, so the move is in the room before we name it.

Walt Whitman, “O Me! O Life!” Week 5 · Wed, Sep 23 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — after all the doubt and the “empty and useless years,” Whitman's answer: “that the powerful play goes on, and you may contribute a verse.” On the last day of Act I, what is the verse that only you can add?

Purpose: the standing ritual — and the note Act I ends on: the play goes on, and your verse is the un-fakeable thing you bring to it.

Act II — The Currere · Weeks 6–10

Linda Pastan, “Ethics” Week 6 · Mon, Sep 28 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem, or hear Pastan read it herself. Her speaker was asked a question in school she couldn't really answer, and only understood it years later, standing in a museum as an old woman herself.
  • Don't get stuck on “ethics class.” When has the meaning of something a teacher asked or said only caught up with you later?

Purpose: that gap — between the moment and its meaning — is what currere is made of. The method is in the room before we name it.

Emily Dickinson, “Tell all the truth but tell it slant” Week 6 · Wed, Sep 30 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — what does she mean by telling the truth “slant” instead of straight out? When have you told something true by coming at it sideways?

Purpose: a currere tells a true thing slant — through scene, not statement. The poem sets that up before we ask you to do it.

Robert Hayden, “Those Winter Sundays” Week 7 · Wed, Oct 7 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — a small remembered scene, and the meaning arrives only at the end: “What did I know, what did I know / of love's austere and lonely offices?” How does one scene carry a whole relationship?

Purpose: a single rendered scene, understood in hindsight — exactly what your storyboard cards are reaching for.

Li-Young Lee, “The Gift” Week 8 · Mon, Oct 12 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — one remembered moment, a father pulling out a splinter, carries a whole life of tenderness. Where does the poem show instead of tell? Notice how it moves between past and present without ever announcing the years.

Purpose: a single moment, fully rendered, doing all the work — exactly what a load-bearing scene in your currere is asked to do.

E. E. Cummings, “[since feeling is first]” Week 8 · Wed, Oct 14 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — watch what he does to grammar on purpose: no capitals, the syntax bent to the feeling, “who pays any attention / to the syntax of things / will never wholly kiss you.” When does breaking the rule tell it truer than the rule would?

Purpose: the syntax-breaker's own argument for breaking syntax — exactly the permission the day's freewrite runs on.

Sylvia Plath, “Metaphors” Week 9 · Mon, Oct 19 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — the whole thing is a riddle built only of images, “an elephant, a ponderous house,” “a melon strolling on two tendrils,” and she never says the plain word. What does saying it in metaphor do that the plain word couldn't?

Purpose: a poem that is its metaphors — the exact move you're hunting for in your own draft that day.

Robert Frost, “The Road Not Taken” Week 10 · Mon, Oct 26 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — the speaker isn't in the woods; he's looking back, “ages and ages hence,” making a story out of a choice: “I shall be telling this with a sigh.” How does meaning get made only in the looking-back?

Purpose: the synthetical move in miniature — a life-thread understood only in hindsight, which is exactly the close you write that day.

Walt Whitman, “Song of Myself, 51” Week 10 · Wed, Oct 28 · ~10 min

  • Read the poem — aloud, it was built to be. “Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)” Your currere is one voice — but how many kinds of writing are packed inside it?

Purpose: a poem meant for the voice, on the day you record yours — and its “I contain multitudes” is the exact hinge into the buried genres.
