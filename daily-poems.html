Daily Readings

TCE 284 · Writing for Educators · Fall 2026

Most sessions open with something read aloud — a poem, a song lyric, once a book — talked about for ten minutes, then left to do its work. Nothing to prepare and nothing to turn in. They are collected here with the question each one opens, so you can find one again afterwards, catch up on a session you missed, or read ahead.

Twenty-six of them, Weeks 1–15. Every title links to the text.

Act I — Become a Writer

Paul Laurence Dunbar

We Wear the Mask

What do you notice? Who is the “we”? What does the mask cost, and what does it protect?

Week 1 · Wed, Aug 26 · poem · Dayton, Ohio · ~5 min

George Ella Lyon

Where I’m From

What do you notice — the lists, the specific names, the foods and sayings? The poem is built to be borrowed. Where are you from?

Week 2 · Mon, Aug 31 · poem · built to be borrowed

Shel Silverstein

Sick

Romano writes about place, risk, seeking surprise, and seeking pleasure in language. Which of those do you see on display in a classic children’s poem?

Week 2 · Wed, Sep 2 · poem · children’s verse

Missing Persons

Words

Read the lyric, then hear it sung. One voice asking whether anybody on the other end is listening. What does the singing do to those words that the page doesn’t?

Week 3 · Wed, Sep 9 · song lyric · 1982 · lyric · hear it sung

Galway Kinnell

Blackberry Eating

Which words make you taste it — “the black art of blackberry-making,” the lumpy words he loves to “squeeze, squinch open, and splurge”? Point to the exact ones.

Week 4 · Mon, Sep 14 · poem · words you can taste

Seamus Heaney

Mid-Term Break

Heaney never says he’s grieving — he shows it: the knelling bells, the baby cooing, the old men standing to shake his hand, and the last line. Where does the grief land?

Week 4 · Wed, Sep 16 · poem · shown, never told

Jim Daniels

Short Order Cook

Where does the poem move fast, break, and refuse to sit still? What rules is it not following — and why does it work anyway?

Week 5 · Mon, Sep 21 · poem · breaks rules in style

Walt Whitman

O Me! O Life!

“That the powerful play goes on, and you may contribute a verse.” On the last day of Act I, what is the verse that only you can add?

Week 5 · Wed, Sep 23 · poem · closes Act I

Act II — The Currere

Linda Pastan

Ethics

Don’t get stuck on “ethics class.” When has the meaning of something a teacher asked or said only caught up with you years later?

Week 6 · Mon, Sep 28 · poem · + the poet reading it · hear Pastan read it

Emily Dickinson

Tell all the truth but tell it slant

What does she mean by telling the truth “slant” instead of straight out? When have you told something true by coming at it sideways?

Week 6 · Wed, Sep 30 · poem · scene, not statement

Naomi Shihab Nye

Valentine for Ernest Mann

“You can’t order a poem like you order a taco.” Poems hide in the ordinary until someone looks. Read it while you wait — then tell me the thread you want to chase.

Week 7 · Mon, Oct 5 · poem · 29 lines · conference day

Robert Hayden

Those Winter Sundays

The meaning arrives only at the end — “What did I know, what did I know / of love’s austere and lonely offices?” How does one scene carry a whole relationship?

Week 7 · Wed, Oct 7 · poem · one scene, whole life

Li-Young Lee

The Gift

One remembered moment — a father pulling out a splinter — carries a whole life of tenderness. Where does the poem show instead of tell?

Week 8 · Mon, Oct 12 · poem · a load-bearing scene

E. E. Cummings

[since feeling is first]

Watch what he does to grammar on purpose: “who pays any attention / to the syntax of things / will never wholly kiss you.” When does breaking the rule tell it truer?

Week 8 · Wed, Oct 14 · poem · syntax bent on purpose

Sylvia Plath

Metaphors

The whole poem is a riddle built only of images — “an elephant, a ponderous house” — and she never says the plain word. What does the metaphor do that the plain word couldn’t?

Week 9 · Mon, Oct 19 · poem · a riddle of images

Leonard Cohen

Anthem

“There is a crack, a crack in everything / That’s how the light gets in.” You are bringing me a draft that is not finished. That is the point of it.

Week 9 · Wed, Oct 21 · song lyric · 1992 · conference day

Robert Frost

The Road Not Taken

The speaker isn’t in the woods — he’s looking back, “ages and ages hence,” making a story out of a choice. How does meaning get made only in the looking-back?

Week 10 · Mon, Oct 26 · poem · made in the looking-back

Walt Whitman

Song of Myself, 51

“Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)” Your currere is one voice — how many kinds of writing are packed inside it?

Week 10 · Wed, Oct 28 · poem · I contain multitudes

Act III — Multimodal Research Project

Walt Whitman

When I Heard the Learn’d Astronomer

The lecture has the charts, the proofs, the columns. He walks out and looks up at the stars in silence. On the day you start researching: what does the summary miss?

Week 11 · Mon, Nov 2 · poem · 8 lines

Tracy Chapman

Fast Car

A whole life — a father, a shelter, a checkout job, a plan — told in four verses and no explanation. You have to gather one source yourself. Whose story has nobody asked for?

Week 11 · Wed, Nov 4 · song lyric · 1988 · lyric

Elizabeth Bishop

Filling Station

A grubby station rendered detail by detail until “Somebody loves us all.” The machine gives you the average station. What does Bishop see that an average could not?

Week 12 · Mon, Nov 9 · poem · 41 lines

Nine Inch Nails · Johnny Cash

Hurt

Same words, two voices: Reznor at 29 writing it, Cash at 70 singing it into something else entirely. On the day you start your transformed artifact — what did the second version do to the first?

Week 12 · Wed, Nov 11 · song lyric · 1994 / 2002 · two versions · Cash’s version

Jane Kenyon

Otherwise

Ordinary mornings, one after another, held together by a line that keeps returning: “But one day, I know, / it will be otherwise.” That repeated line is a unifying element doing its job.

Week 13 · Mon, Nov 16 · poem · 26 lines

Dolly Parton

Coat of Many Colors

A coat sewn out of rags and scraps that nobody else valued, and it holds together as one thing — because of how it was put together, and who put it there. That is your multimodal project.

Week 13 · Wed, Nov 18 · song lyric · 1971 · lyric

Talking Heads

Once in a Lifetime

“And you may ask yourself — well, how did I get here?” You are writing your reflective note today: the looking-back that turns a pile of work into a story about how you got here.

Week 15 · Mon, Nov 30 · song lyric · 1980 · lyric

Dr. Seuss

Oh, the Places You’ll Go!

“You have brains in your head. You have feet in your shoes. You can steer yourself any direction you choose.” The last day of the term, after you have shown the room what you made.

Week 15 · Wed, Dec 2 · read from the book · 1990 · read from the book
