Grammar B — Four Moves, with Examples

TCE 284 · Week 5 · Break the Rules in Style (Weathers / Romano, WWM ch15) — be bad on purpose

Four ways to break "Grammar A" for effect. For each move: read the examples, name what it's doing, then take two minutes to try it. The first examples under each move come from the chapters you just read — Write What Matters ch14 and ch15, page numbers given so you can find them. After those: a public-domain classic or two, and student examples (initials only) showing the same move by writers your students' age.

1 · Sentence Fragments

Isolate an idea. Cut it loose from the sentence and let it land on its own. Romano: "perfect for isolating an important idea. For focusing readers' attention on one thing. For pointing up a reversal or dramatic outcome." (p. 66 — and notice he does it while saying it.)

From the reading — WWM ch14–15

Holly Jeric, a college freshman, quoted in WWM p. 66

… and you are basically at odds with life because things are going wrong and the future can only hold unpleasant surprises, someone smiles at you. Like warmth. Like yellow in the gray. It's amazing.

A labyrinthine sentence piles up the bad morning, a full sentence delivers the smile — then two fragments land it. The fragments are the point of the whole paragraph.

Tom Romano, "Bulldog Learning," WWM ch14, p. 61

The teacher's role is important. In some cases, critical.

A radio commentary, so the fragment is the pause you'd hear on air. Romano fragments his own prose all through both chapters — "Ho hum, I say. So what?" (p. 59); "Rules change, open up." (p. 69); and the two words the chapter is built on: Who says?

Melissa Podgorski, a college student, quoted in WWM p. 64

Before college, I never broke any of them. No sentence fragments.
No one sentence paragraphs.

She lists the rules she was never allowed to break — and breaks each one in the sentence that names it.

Classic + student examples

Charles Dickens, Bleak House (1853) — public domain

London. Michaelmas Term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln's Inn Hall. Implacable November weather. … Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city.

Dickens opens a whole novel on fragments — London. … Implacable November weather. — then hammers the word Fog until the city disappears into it.

"Cat" — M.H., grade 12 (de-identified)

I. Once. No. Many times. Tried to ignore it. But woke still. With my cat. Walking. On my chest. Licking. My face.

2-minute try: take one ordinary moment and write it in fragments only. No complete sentences.

2 · Labyrinthine Sentence

One long, weaving, controlled sentence for something that goes on and on — "think labyrinth, not maze" (p. 65): in a maze you get lost; in a labyrinth the path leads somewhere. You hold the period until the very end.

From the reading — WWM ch15

Tom Wolfe, The Right Stuff, quoted in WWM pp. 65–66

To take off in an F-100 at dawn and cut in the afterburner and hurtle twenty-five thousand feet up into the sky so suddenly that you felt not like a bird but like a trajectory, yet with full control, full control of five tons of thrust, all of which flowed from your will and through your fingertips, with the huge engine right beneath you, so close that it was as if you were riding it bareback, until you leveled out and went supersonic … (keep reading in the book — it's one sentence to the end)

Romano's pick for "something of ongoingness: intense experiences, emotions, dreams, deep, urgent passions." The sentence doesn't stop because the pilot doesn't.

Holly Jeric, quoted in WWM p. 66

On those cloudy days when it's drizzling and you are running late so you can't grab your umbrella, and although it seems warmer, you somehow slip on a patch of unforeseen ice while rushing to get to the place you need to go, wishing you believed in shoes with traction and not style, and you are basically at odds with life because things are going wrong and the future can only hold unpleasant surprises, someone smiles at you.

The same passage as in move 1 — the labyrinth is the bad morning, and it only ends when someone smiles. Same writer, two moves, one paragraph.

Tom Romano, WWM p. 65 — doing it while describing it

Your words carry readers on a roller-coaster ride, hurtling and careening, dipping and rising, nearly out of control but not, since you are the one guiding the ride, braking only when you provide the period.

Classic + student examples

Charles Dickens, A Tale of Two Cities (1859) — public domain

It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair…

One sentence, riding parallel construction — it was… it was… it was… — until the rhythm itself becomes the meaning.

"Waiting for the Bus" — J.A., grade 11 (de-identified)

Opens on fragments — Waiting. For the bus. — then runs on and on through a frostbitten nose, a scarf blowing away, coughing, the bus lurching forward, all the way to one blunt word at the end: CHARTERED. The length is the wait.

2-minute try: describe something tedious or endless in a single sentence you refuse to end.

3 · Purposeful Misspelling

Bend or respell a word so the spelling itself carries meaning or voice. Romano's two kinds (pp. 67–68): respelling to capture how someone actually talks, and inventing or altering a word "for meaningful effect." Every "error" is one you meant.

From the reading — WWM ch15

Tom Romano, WWM p. 68 — the typo he kept

"I accidentally wrote once that someone self-satisfied and smug had become a victim of his own wisdoom. I appreciated the typo so much that I used it as a purposeful respelling."

Wednesday you'll watch the machine turn wisdoom back into wisdom. That is the whole lab in one word.

e. e. cummings and Jeanie Tomasko, quoted in WWM p. 68

cummings on spring: mud-luscious and puddle-wonderful. Tomasko on people walking about in April: "unburdened unbuttoned and umbrella'd."

Not misspellings so much as words that didn't exist until the writer needed them.

John Dos Passos on Henry Ford's assembly line, quoted in WWM p. 68

… reachunder, adjustwasher, screwdown bolt, shove in cotterpin, reachunder, screwdown bolt, reachunderadjustscrewdownreachunderadjust until every ounce of life was sucked off into production …

The words run together the way the motions do. Spelling as monotony.

Harry Crews, A Childhood, quoted in WWM p. 68 — a south Georgia faith healer, 1940

"Now the thing about far is you don't want to drive it no deeper. That's how come it is doctors cain't do no good with burns is they drive the far deeper. But me, what I do is draw it out, draw that far right on out of there. But a doctor he will most gently drive that heat right down to the bones, drive it even to the holler of the body."

Respelling for voice: far is fire, holler is hollow. You hear him. (Romano's other dialect example, on p. 67, is Jim's speech in Huckleberry Finn — read it there.)

Classic + student examples

Mark Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) — public domain

"You don't know about me without you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer; but that ain't no matter. … The Widow Douglas she took me for her son, and allowed she would sivilize me."

Huck's whole voice lives in the "wrong" grammar and spelling — ain't no matter, sivilize — a boy who's never been to school, spelled onto the page exactly as he'd say it.

"Spring Break" — E.K., grade 12 (de-identified)

Ends by loading its misspellings with double meaning: seven daze of onederful sites, clear beautiful sees — "days/daze," "wonderful/one-der-ful," "scenes/sees" all at once.

2-minute try: respell three words so the misspelling means something the correct spelling can't.

4 · Double Voice

Two voices at once, set side by side — the reader hears both, and the gap between them is the point.

From the reading — WWM ch14 (the nearest cousin)

Tom Romano, the Dunkin' Donuts scene, WWM ch14 pp. 59–60

"Don't be impatient. The line's moving fast."
"It's not moving at all," said the girl. "I don't even want a doughnut."
…
"That's not breakfast," Mom said, fluttering her eyelids.
The girl pounced. "But a doughnut is?"

Ch14–15 don't have a true double-voice piece, but this is its cousin. Dramatic narrative: two voices, one exchange, no agreement — and they take turns. Double voice is what happens when you stop taking turns and let them run side by side.

Student example + a model to hear

Untitled two-voice piece — S.R., grade 12 (de-identified)

Girl friend . . . Girl friend I love you. Only one for me Always and forever. Who's she? Always be together. Is she new here? Spend eternity together. Do you know what her name is?

One voice vows eternity; the indented second voice keeps asking who she even is. Two people, one exchange, no agreement.

Modern model to hear read aloud (still in copyright, so not reprinted here): Paul Fleischman, "Fireflies," from Joyful Noise: Poems for Two Voices — printed in two columns for two readers, lines sometimes solo, sometimes spoken together.

2-minute try: write a short exchange where a second voice (indented) interrupts the first — doubt, memory, or a whole other person. Or take the Dunkin' scene and stop letting them take turns.
