2 · Reading discussion — O'Hara + Lamott (~15 min)

O'Hara, "Moviehouse Poems (3)" (Currere Exchange Journal, 2025 — also on Canvas). Eight poems, each built on a movie or a moviehouse. He teaches at Badin, in Hamilton.

Up front: this isn't the Grammar B chapter in poem form. It's a different kind of rule-breaking — a poet who knows the forms and bends them — and it's also a currere: a whole life told through what he watched. Both matter today.

  1. Read "In the Projection Room" aloud (~2 min). The platter isn't a record: in a projection booth the whole film sits on a big horizontal steel platter and feeds off it through the projector. "The platter spins / and light floats / above us" is the movie starting.
  2. Pairs, one poem each (~5 min). Eight poems, so every pair gets one. Find one rule your poem breaks and say what it buys. Sound, line breaks, rhyme, a sentence that won't end, a "mistake." Mark the spot.
  3. Round the room (~4 min). One find per pair.
  4. Then Lamott (~3 min). "Shitty First Drafts": what permission is she giving? Now look at O'Hara's plot summaries — "The plot: Clark Gable's a jewel thief." They're flat, almost on purpose. Is that a shitty first draft left in? Or is the flat summary the "movie," and the poem happens around it?
  5. One line to close (~1 min). What's O'Hara's thread — the one thing he could tell his whole life through? Hold onto that. Wednesday you go looking for yours.
Where the rules bend — if your pair is stuck
  • A sentence that won't end. "Now, Voyager": from "From a back row" to "wondering if any ending can be happy" is one sentence. "In the Projection Room" is one sentence start to finish. The sentence does what the movie does — carries you off.
  • Two voices at once. "One Minute to Zero" runs two things side by side with no transitions: the plot ("Mitchum's deployed to South Korea…") and the pond outside ("Petals from a dogwood / float on the pond"). What is the pond doing there? Who's watching whom?
  • Fragments. "The plot: Clark Gable's a jewel thief." "The rub?"
  • A form kept and broken. "Marathon Man: Rhyme Royal" keeps the seven-line ababbcc scheme and then rhymes white with white-, and splits "hard-/pack" and "white-/hot" across the line. "They Met in Bombay" is fourteen lines on slant rhymes (thief / league / relief / intrigue) and one in two languages (Chang / drang). "A Course in Being a Spectator" ends line after line on sight and light — the don't-repeat-yourself rule, broken until the repeating is the point.
  • Telling on purpose? Last week was Show, Don't Tell. The whole Cary Grant poem is a plot summary. Opposite move — and is it a choice?
  • Small errors — or are they? Capitals in the middle of sentences ("Held guns," "The crowd's"), the "Eve/Eva's" slash, the exclamation at Rita Hayworth. Which are on purpose? That's the standard for Breaking the Rules: every error one you meant. Wednesday the machine will "fix" all of these.
Who's who — the movies and the people, if you need them
  • They Met in Bombay (1941). Clark Gable — the biggest male star of the studio era (Gone with the Wind), "the King of Hollywood." Rosalind Russell — fast-talking comic lead (His Girl Friday). Peter Lorre — Hungarian-born character actor with bulging eyes and a soft, sinister voice (M, Casablanca). Here he plays a Chinese ship's captain, and "eyes taped" means exactly that: yellowface makeup. O'Hara records it flatly and ends the poem with "(Deceit has always been applauded)" — the jewel thief, the fake officer, the medal, and the taped eyes all land under that line.
  • One Minute to Zero (1952). Korean War. Robert Mitchum — heavy-lidded, laconic leading man of the 40s–70s (Out of the Past, The Night of the Hunter, Cape Fear). Ann Blyth — Veda, the daughter in Mildred Pierce.
  • Marathon Man (1976). Dustin Hoffman (The Graduate, Rain Man) as a grad student; the famous scene is Laurence Olivier — the great Shakespearean, here a Nazi war criminal — torturing him in a dentist's chair: "Is it safe?" Roy Scheider — Chief Brody in Jaws ("You're gonna need a bigger boat") — plays Hoffman's brother, a government agent. "Stuck in the hard-pack of his belly, face a pre-ghost white" is his death: stabbed, staggering to his brother's door.
  • House of Wax (1953). Vincent Price, the horror icon; his wax face cracks and falls away. Shot in 3D — the "gimmick" that didn't survive. The Sam Eric was a big Philadelphia movie palace.
  • Cary Grant's Co-Stars. Ingrid Bergman in Notorious (1946): undercover among Nazis; Grant won't admit he loves her until she's being poisoned. Eva Marie Saint in North by Northwest (1959): she's Eve Kendall, hence "Eve/Eva"; the climax is on a replica Mount Rushmore; "no strangers on the train" winks at another Hitchcock. Audrey Hepburn in Charade (1963): Grant keeps changing his name; the fortune turns out to be rare postage stamps — "placed her stamp."
  • Cinema as Redemption. A wartime summer matinee. Spy Smasher was a 1942 serial. "She enters" is Rita Hayworth in Gilda (1946) — the hair-flip entrance, one of the most famous in film.
  • Now, Voyager (1942). Bette Davis, the biggest star at Warner Bros., as a repressed Boston spinster who comes into her own. Claude Rains — Captain Renault in Casablanca — is the psychiatrist; the "burnished bass" is right. (The famous two-cigarettes scene is Paul Henreid, the lover, not Rains.) The film's last line, "Don't let's ask for the moon. We have the stars," is the happy ending the poem doubts.

Purpose: prime the mindset — play, permission, "be bad now and then" — and meet a currere before we name the method next week.
