Week 2 · Wednesday — Build the page

Act I · Writing Place · what does your place look, sound, and feel like?
Today's goals: Post your writing-place photos to the class board, loosen up with Lamott and take Romano's dare to write without holding back, sharpen your details until we can see them, and compose OP2 — words and photos on one page, with music on. Due Friday.

0 · Daily Poem — "I dwell in Possibility" (~7 min)

  • Emily Dickinson, "I dwell in Possibility –". Read it aloud. She calls poetry a house — "A fairer House than Prose – / More numerous of Windows –." What kind of house is your writing?

Purpose: the standing ritual — the writer's place as a dwelling, on the day you build your own.

1 · Housekeeping + post your photos (~5 min)

  • Right now, from your phone: post your writing-place photos to our class Padlet — TODO: paste Padlet link. Two or three shots, including one of you at work. Put your name on the post.
  • No photos? No problem today — the board already holds a column of Jill Krementz's The Writer's Desk portraits (the book Romano built this assignment from). Claim one of those to work from, and shoot your own tonight.
  • Questions on OP2.

Purpose: get every image onto one screen we can all see, in the first five minutes, without anyone needing to have uploaded ahead of time.

2 · Permission and the dare — Lamott + Romano ch6–8 (~15 min)

  • Permission first. Watch Anne Lamott's writing tips. As you watch, jot one thing she says about how drafting really works — you'll use it in thirty seconds. (Her "let it be bad" comes back in Week 5 with the essay.)
  • Then the dare. Lamott gives you permission for the first pass to be terrible. Romano asks for something harder in the same breath — that you spend everything you have on it anyway. Hold both today.
  • Risk (ch6). Romano pushes Kacie on a piece about being caught shoplifting: "If you're going to write about this experience, Kacie, you've got to go all the way. You've got to describe it honestly with detail." She says, "You're killing me." Pairs: why was he killing her — and was he right to push?
  • Annie Dillard's rule, which Romano quotes: "spend it all, shoot it, play it, lose it, all, right away, every time. Do not hoard what seems good for a later place." At a spoken-word festival, after a student's stirring performance, the poet Jim Flanagan told the room: "There's courage on the athletic field, and we don't diminish that. But what we've experienced today, this is the sport of the soul." Not every piece has to be your hardest story — but whatever you pick, don't hold back on it. (Imagine a room that got as competitive about poetry as it does about football.)
  • Whole group, fast: ch7 says seek surprise — move "in a direction the reader doesn't expect." Ch8 says take pleasure in the words themselves ("Walmart is not the only saving place"). Where's the risk, the surprise, or the pleasure in a page about a desk?

Purpose: loosen the grip and raise the stakes before composing — Lamott says it's allowed to be bad, Romano says don't hold back. Length is as long as it needs to be, correctness comes later, significance is the whole game.

3 · Gallery walk — the class writing places (~12 min)

  • Silent walk (3 min). Scroll the Padlet on your own screen. Comment on two posts that aren't yours — and the comment must name one concrete thing you can actually see. "Nice setup!" doesn't count. "The mug is holding down a stack of paper" counts.
  • Trios (6 min). Pull up your own post. Say what your place needs — and what surprised you about seeing yourself at work. Romano reads the Krementz photos with four questions; use them on each other: What's in the frame? What isn't? What is the writer doing? What angle and distance was it shot from?
  • Whole room (3 min). Hands up: where's the weirdest place you have ever written? Romano's friend drafted a published poem in a hotel bathtub after midnight; Stephen King wrote two novels in the laundry room of a double-wide, a child's desk balanced on his thigh; Romano drove across town on long weekends to write in a dark, empty, air-conditioned classroom.

Purpose: seeing others' places sharpens your own — and the comment rule makes everyone practice the concrete noticing before they're asked to write it.

4 · Imagery — the pencil test, then write it (~20 min)

  • Mini-lesson (4 min) — the pencil test. Romano could have written: "In a coffee mug I keep pens, markers, highlighters, scissors, and pencils." Here's what he wrote instead:
    In a coffee mug filled with pens, markers, highlighters, and pair of scissors, stand half a dozen Dixon Tri-Conderoga No. 2 HB pencils. They are black with soft graphite. Even the eraser is black. The shaft is triangular and thicker than standard pencils. They're good to grip.
    What does the second version give you that the first doesn't? Name it: a brand, a color, a shape, a reason — and a writer who cares about pencils. That's the whole move.
  • And one from a student — same assignment, your seat:
    These tables stand by my side like a trusty dog, encouraging me to write. I am a lone knight of my own round table. My sword, a pen. I bleed black ink.
    Name the devices: the dog is a simile, the knight is a metaphor. And the student whose IKEA desk "snuggles up next to the foot of my bed" is using personification. (Metaphor gets a whole day in Week 9.)
  • Count off 1–2–3. Your number is your subject. Write it for 6 minutes in Journaler — no general words, name the actual thing:
    1. Sound and light. What do you hear when you write, and what's the light? Romano wants silence "save the voice in my head"; King writes inside heavy metal; Murray wrote to loud classical; one student matched the music to the subject — Italian opera for Latin class, German folk music for German folktales — and burned a scented candle.
    2. Time and ritual. When do you write, and what has to happen first? Romano starts before dawn and breaks to brew a French press — "I like the ritual as well as the caffeine lift." Liz writes at 10 p.m. or later: "On some nights, at 4 a.m., you can see the faint glow of light from in between the blinds… If you listen hard enough, you can hear the sounds of typing."
    3. Objects and creatures. What's within arm's reach — tools, talismans, pets? Romano has the Tri-Conderoga pencils, a widemouthed ceramic frog full of paper clips that a high school student made him, and a schnauzer named Minnie Mae who waits on the stair landing. Hillary photographed an orange tabby with one paw on her keyboard. (Ask Todd about the Iron Sheik on the desk.)
    One requirement, from the mentor texts: land at least one simile, metaphor, or bit of personification.
  • Trios (6 min) — one 1, one 2, one 3. Read your lines aloud. Listeners: say back the single most concrete detail you heard, and where you wanted a sharper one. You leave holding all three subjects.
  • Whole room (4 min). Three or four read one line. Then Romano's ch7 question: did anything surprise you as you wrote it?

Purpose: everyone writes, everyone talks, and no two people in a trio brought the same material — so the sharing is an exchange, not a round of repeats. This is the language OP2 needs, and it plants the senses OP3 builds on.

5 · Compose OP2 in Journaler — with music (~21 min)

  • Music on. You just wrote about sound; now test it. Instrumental plays in the room — bring your own in earbuds if lyrics don't distract you, or work in silence like Romano. We'll ask at the end which one you actually wanted.
  • Build your Writing Place One-Pager: your observations + 2–3 photos (one of you at work), one page. Pull the strongest lines from Monday's gush and today's quick-write. Spend it all — don't save the good detail for later.
  • Trade with a neighbor for one read: what do they see clearly, and where do they want a sharper detail?

Purpose: get most of the page down in the room, with a reader and a soundtrack, so the take-home is polish, not a blank start.

Wrap-up & at home:
  • Quick round: music, earbuds, or silence — which one did you want? That's a real finding about your writing place; put it in the One-Pager.
  • Romano's last word on the one-page limit: "You can't write about everything. Deepen your commitment to what you keep." And his counterweight, from Natalie: "I could recreate this desk anywhere because it is the mental state that I value more than the tangible place where I sit and write."
