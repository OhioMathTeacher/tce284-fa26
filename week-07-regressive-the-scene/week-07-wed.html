Week 7 · Wednesday — Storyboard Your Scenes

Act II · currere · two past · two present · two future
Today's goals: Turn your thread into six scenes that reach across time, then write your way into each one.

0 · Daily Poem — "Those Winter Sundays" (~10 min)
  • Robert Hayden, "Those Winter Sundays". Read it aloud. A small remembered scene, and the meaning arrives only at the end — "What did I know, what did I know of love's austere and lonely offices?" How does one scene carry a whole relationship?

Purpose: a single rendered scene, understood in hindsight — exactly what your storyboard cards are reaching for.

1 · News, the conferences, and how this is going (~10 min)
  • Any news? Something you made, something that happened, something you read and couldn't put down. Two or three people, that's all.
  • Monday's conferences — how did they land? What was useful, what you wish we had done with the fifteen minutes, what you walked out still unsure about. Say it plainly; I would rather hear it now, while there is still time to change how we run the second round.
  • And the currere itself. Where are you with it — the gushes, the thread, the Guidelines? Who is still circling a topic? That is not a confession, it is useful information, and today is built to help.
  • Missed the conference, or the packet didn't get in? Catch me on the way out and we will find fifteen minutes this week. You are not behind in any way that matters yet.
  • Your writing can go somewhere besides my grade book. Two of these take exactly what you are drafting right now — and published undergraduate writer is a real line on a teaching résumé.
    • Inklings Arts & Letters — Miami's undergraduate literary magazine. Any kind of writing, any kind of art, up to five pieces, your name stripped off the file for anonymous reading. Writing to inklingswriting@miamioh.edu, art to inklingsart@miamioh.edu. They answer in a week or two and they are always looking for staff. Their one rule: nothing written with AI. Your gushes are your own sentences — keep them that way and this door stays open.
    • Happy Captive — also undergraduate, also annual, and it names creative nonfiction outright, which is what a currere is. Up to five pieces to hcmsubmissions@miamioh.edu, .docx or Google Docs (not PDFs), name off the work, year and genre in the email. Deadline falls in early February, so a piece you finish this month has months to sit.
    • Also at Miami: The Femellectual (this year's theme is "Flight Risk"), Oxford Magazine (MFA-edited, open to undergraduates), and The Miami Student, which takes guest columns — I had one in it in August, so I can tell you exactly how that goes. The library's list has the rest.
    • Past Miami: Chill Subs — five thousand-odd magazines you can filter by what they want, whether they charge a reading fee, and how long they take to answer, with a tracker for what you have sent where. One of you asked me this a few weeks ago and this is where I sent her. Start there rather than googling "where to publish."

Purpose: ask how the process is landing while it can still be changed, and let the people who are stuck hear that they are not the only ones.

2 · Drawing it out (WWM ch18) (~10 min)
  • You read ch18, "Drawing It Out." With a partner: what does Romano say drawing does that outlining doesn't? Why might a picture find a scene your sentences would miss?

Purpose: give them permission to draw before they write — the picture surfaces the moment.

3 · Storyboard — six scenes across time (~30 min)
  • Take six index cards. Cards, large sheets and tape are on the table — take what you need. On each card, draw a single scene from your thread — a moment, not a summary. Rough sketches; no one's grading the art.
  • Spread them across time — and these are the moments you named last week:
    • Regressive — two past. Two you lived as a learner or a young teacher.
    • Analytical — two present. Two from your life now. This is the in-between Moore talks about: the space between where you have been and where you are going, which is where you are standing.
    • Progressive — two future. Two you can imagine ahead. Build them out of the past cards, the way last week's second gush did.
  • Six cards is not six sections. These are the material, not the outline. A braided currere may fold three of these into a single paragraph.
  • Now lay them out and re-sequence. Timeline order is rarely the strongest one. Try it two ways, because this is you choosing the shape:
    • One line — the six in a single row, in the order that reads truest. That is the open shape: named parts, one after another, like "Beyond Presence."
    • Two rows and a zig-zag — past cards along the top, future cards beneath, then trace the reading order between the rows with your finger. That is braiding: you cut away from a scene and come back to it. It is how "Who's Cheating Whom?" works — the looking-back happens inside a scene, not in a section of its own.
    Keep whichever picture makes your thread feel true. You are allowed to change your mind while drafting.

Purpose: force the thread across all three times before drafting, so the future and present don't get lost behind one vivid past — and make the open/braided choice something they do with their hands rather than something they read about.

4 · Write around the card (~20 min)
  • Tape your six cards onto a large sheet of paper. For each card, set a timer and write around it for a few minutes — what happened, what it looked and sounded like, what you notice now.
  • Let it spill into the margins. As Romano puts it, writing is expansive, literally. Don't polish. Get the scene down.

Purpose: turn each drawn moment into raw prose while the picture is in front of you — the seed of every scene in the draft.

Wrap-up & over the weekend:
  • Turn your six cards into six rough scene drafts — a paragraph or two each, still messy. Bring them Monday. This is the material your First Draft is built from.
