Week 9 · Monday — Read It Aloud: Get Real Readers

Act II · currere · the whole draft, on real readers
Today's goals: Hear your whole first draft aloud, name the ONE thing you most want help with, and get two readers on the whole piece: the thread, where they got lost, the close, and your ONE thing.

0 · Daily Poem — "Metaphors" (~10 min)

  • Sylvia Plath, "Metaphors". Read it aloud. The whole poem is a riddle built only of images — "an elephant, a ponderous house," "a melon strolling on two tendrils" — and she never says the plain word. What does saying it in metaphor do that the plain word couldn't?

Purpose: a poem that is its metaphors. One image can carry a whole thread.

1 · Ledes & metaphor (WWM ch16–17) (~5 min)

  • You read ch16, "Metaphor" and ch17, "Leding the Way." One quick question each: does your opening start, or clear its throat first? Is there one image in your draft that could carry the thread?
  • Keep them in your pocket. If your lede or an image turns out to be your ONE thing, your group can work it.

Purpose: two tools from the reading, ready if you need them. Today's main job is bigger: the whole draft.

2 · Read it aloud, name your ONE thing (~10 min)

  • Read your own first draft aloud, quietly, to yourself. Your ear catches what your eye skips. Mark where you stumble, and where you lose interest yourself.
  • On the peer review handout, write your ONE thing: a place or a move you can point to, not "is it good?" (My close just summarizes. The cafeteria scene feels flat. Does the jump from my present to my future make sense?)

Purpose: hear your own draft first, and walk into your group knowing what you need from it.

3 · Peer review in threes (~55 min)

  • Find your group. Open Links to Draft Curreres (sign in with your Miami account). Shaded rows are groups. Open the other two writers' Docs. Not on the sheet yet? Add your row now, and share your Doc as Anyone with the link · Commenter.
  • Read and comment (15 min). Read both drafts start to finish, silently. Leave at least one comment in each Doc for each lens:
    1. The thread. Can you feel one thread through all six scenes? Does it reach the present and the future, not just the past?
    2. Where you lost me. A gap between two scenes, a jump that didn't make sense, a place you stopped caring. Point to it.
    3. The close. Does it make new meaning of the thread, or just stop? Is it a real try at the synthetical close, or a summary?
    Say what's working too, in specifics: a line you'll remember, a scene that put you in the room.
  • Talk, one writer at a time (~13 min each). The writer names their ONE thing and reads aloud the part it lives in. The two readers respond: first to the ONE thing, then what they saw on the three lenses. The writer listens and takes notes on the handout. No defending, just "can you say more?"
  • Each writer leaves with their group's answer on their ONE thing and one or two moves to try before tomorrow night.

Purpose: real readers on the whole draft before the machine and before your conference. The comments stay in your Doc; the handout holds what was said out loud.

Wrap-up & before your conference:
  • Revise from your group: the move or two you left with, and anything in your Doc's comments you agree with.
  • Then run Todd-in-a-Can — human readers first, the machine second. Its prompt is on the First Draft guideline.
  • Sharpen your ONE thing from what your group and Todd-in-a-Can said. It may have changed, and that's fine. The booking form asks for it.
