Week 9 · Monday — Read It Aloud: Get Real Readers

Act II · currere · the whole draft, on real readers
Today's goals: Try new openings that grab a reader from the first line, hear your whole first draft aloud, name the ONE thing you most want help with, and get two readers on the whole piece: the opening, the thread, where they got lost, the close, and your ONE thing.

Housekeeping (~5 min)

  • A classmate is joining us on Zoom today. We need one volunteer with a laptop to connect with them and bring them into a group of three for peer review.
  • No class Wednesday. Instead, sign up for a 15-minute draft conference on Zoom: Thursday Oct 22, 4:00–7:00, or Friday Oct 23, noon–4:00. Sign up here.

Then open Links to Draft Curreres (sign in with your Miami account) and add your row: name, title of your currere, link. I'll shade the rows into groups of three as you sign up. Not done by the time we start? Finish during the read-aloud.

1 · The lede: grab us from the first line (WWM ch17) (~20 min) · slides (private link)

  • Before & after (10 min). Five ledes that clear their throat, and the same five revised. For each one: would you keep reading? How would you fix it? Then see the revision and name the move.
  • Lede lab (10 min). At the very top of your Doc, gush two new openings, 5 minutes each. Don't fix, keep moving. Pick two:
    • Drop us in the middle of a scene, already happening.
    • Open with a line someone actually said.
    • Open with one image that holds your thread.
    • Say the thread in one sentence that stings.
    • Start in the future, then turn back.
    Label them Opening A and Opening B, above your current one. Your readers will tell you which one pulls them in.

Purpose: the first lines decide whether a reader keeps going. Don't guess which opening works; try two and let real readers choose.

2 · Read it aloud, name your ONE thing (~10 min)

  • Read your own first draft aloud, quietly, to yourself. Your ear catches what your eye skips. Mark where you stumble, and where you lose interest yourself.
  • On the peer review handout, write your ONE thing: a place or a move you can point to, not "is it good?" (My close just summarizes. The cafeteria scene feels flat. Does the jump from my present to my future make sense?)

Purpose: hear your own draft first, and walk into your group knowing what you need from it.

3 · Peer review in threes (~45 min: 15 per writer)

  • Find your group. Back on Links to Draft Curreres, shaded rows are groups. Sit together and open the other two writers' Docs. Can you comment? If not, the writer fixes their sharing now.
  • Three rounds, one writer at a time, 15 minutes each.
  • Name it (1 min). The writer says their ONE thing and where it lives in the draft.
  • Read and comment (7 min). The two readers read the writer's draft start to finish, silently, and leave at least one comment in the Doc for each lens:
    1. The opening. Which opening (A, B, or the original) pulls you in? Where did you start paying attention?
    2. The thread. Can you feel one thread through all six scenes? Does it reach the present and the future, not just the past?
    3. Where you lost me. A gap between two scenes, a jump that didn't make sense, a place you stopped caring. Point to it.
    4. The close. Does it make new meaning of the thread, or just stop? Is it a real try at the synthetical close, or a summary?
    Say what's working too, in specifics: a line you'll remember, a scene that put you in the room.
  • Talk (7 min). The two readers respond: first to the ONE thing, then what they saw on the four lenses. The writer listens and takes notes on the handout. No defending, just "can you say more?"
  • Each writer leaves with their group's answer on their ONE thing and one or two moves to try before tomorrow night.

Purpose: real readers on the whole draft before the machine and before your conference. The comments stay in your Doc; the handout holds what was said out loud.
