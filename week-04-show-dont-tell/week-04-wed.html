Week 4 · Wednesday — Show or tell?

Act I · Make Me Taste It · can you tell the difference — and fix it in someone else's page?
Today's goals: Read Goldberg's two pages on showing, rank passages two, then three, then six from most showing to most telling and see the room's answer, read the OP4 assignment guidelines closely — then gush a moment without its big word and start the page. Due Friday.

0 · Daily Poem — "Mid-Term Break" (~10 min)
  • Seamus Heaney, "Mid-Term Break". Read it aloud. Heaney never says he's grieving — he shows it: the knelling bells, the baby cooing, the old men standing to shake his hand, and the last line. Where does the grief land, and how does he get it there without naming it?

Purpose: the standing ritual — and the day's move at its highest level: an emotion shown, never told.

1 · Goldberg: "Don't Tell, but Show" (~10 min)
  • Open Natalie Goldberg, "Don't Tell, but Show" (Writing Down the Bones, two pages). Read it aloud around the room, a paragraph each. Goldberg's four tips for showing, not telling:
    1. Tip 1: Don't write the big words. Anger, honesty, truth, hate, love, sorrow, life, justice. Show us what made you angry; we'll feel angry.
    2. Tip 2: "About" is an alarm. "This story is about my grandmother" — skip that line and go right into the kitchen.
    3. Tip 3: Be there. A story you heard secondhand and never entered comes out "two times removed." Breathe life into it or don't write it.
    4. Tip 4: A general statement is fine — if a concrete picture backs it. Her softer rule, and the one you'll actually use.
  • Which of Goldberg's qualities do you see in Seamus Heaney's poem?
  • Which will you be most mindful of as you write today, and for OP4 — Show, Don't Tell?

Purpose: the day's move named by a writer who says it plainly — and a word to search for and a list to circle when you check your own draft in step 5.

2 · Show or tell? (~20 min)
  • Three rounds — two passages, then three, then all six. Most showing on top, most telling on the bottom. Argue before you drag; submit once per round.
  • After each round we stop and put every pair's order on the screen, side by side, no names. Are we still thinking the same? Let's discuss.
  • Round 1 is the same kitchen written twice — just learn the drag. Round 2 is where the arguing starts. Round 3 adds one passage: count its concrete nouns, then ask whether a single one makes you feel anything.
  • Pairs, one phone. Open each round only when we get there:
    1. Round 1 · two
    2. Round 2 · three
    3. Round 3 · all six

Purpose: see telling in someone else's words first — it's easier than seeing it in your own — and find out whether the room thinks detail alone can carry a feeling.

3 · OP4 — Show, Don't Tell: the assignment guidelines (~10 min)
  • Open the OP4 Show, Don't Tell assignment guidelines together — one small moment from your life, rendered through the senses, one page. No dialogue: if someone is there, we know them by what they do. No summary words.
  • Read the assessment table against what you just did: "shows, never tells" is the sort you just ran, turned on your own page. "The opposite of melon and ham" is passage A next to passage B.
  • Ask now — what's unclear about the page, the "no dialogue" rule, or the grading?

Purpose: read the assignment after the reps, not before — so every line of it points at something you've just done.

4 · Gush the moment, then share (~15 min)
  • The big-word gush (~5 min): pick one word from Goldberg's list — anger, love, sorrow, hate, fear, joy, shame, relief — and one small moment when you felt it. Start the 5-minute timer and write the moment. The word itself is banned. So is "about." Stay on the surface: what the room looked like, what someone's hands did, what you heard, what was on your tongue. If a reader can name your word without being told, you've done it. Gush mode locks editing.
  • After the buzzer (~3 min): answer the app's few questions about how it went.
  • Share (~7 min): read your gush to a neighbor without saying your word. They guess it — then tell you two things: the one line that gave it away, and the one place they were told instead of shown. Then the question that matters: what from this should I develop further? Circle it.

Purpose: Goldberg's first tip as a game — the feeling has to arrive without its name — and a reader's fast read on what's already working before you build.

5 · Start Show, Don't Tell (~15 min)
  • Shape the page in Journaler from what you circled — one small moment, one page, all senses. Aim to leave with most of a page, so the take-home is polish, not a blank start.
  • Cutting a telling word leaves a hole — fill it with one of the five tools at the bottom of Round 3: mind pictures, sensory detail, precise words, pump up the verbs, weed the garden.
  • Before you leave, Goldberg's two tests: search your draft for about — every hit is an alarm. Then circle every big word — happy, sad, nervous, amazing, special, free — and ask whether a picture is standing behind it. If not, the picture goes in and the word comes out.

Purpose: get the page started while the models and your reader's notes are still in the room, and leave with a two-step check you can run at home.
