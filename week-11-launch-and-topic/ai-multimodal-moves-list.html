The AI Multimodal Moves List

A companion to Tom Romano's genre list, for the AI-multimodal work of the project

Romano's list names forms (the obituary, the recipe, the letter). This one names moves — things you do with what a machine produces.

You are welcome (actually ENCOURAGED) to use AI-generated content in your project. The "trick" is to use the content to motivate your OWN writing, your OWN creation. The six sections below provide strategies for incorporating your own work alongside AI-generated content. You do this to create content "that counts".

1 · React & Talk Back

Put your own voice in dialogue with what the machine produced.

  • Recorded reaction. Generate an Audio or Video Overview of your sources in Gemini Notebook, play an excerpt, and record yourself responding — agreeing, arguing, correcting it against your primary source. The focus is the exchange, not the overview. (What counts: your recorded response. The overview does not.)
  • Director's commentary. Record your own narration over a genAI piece, walking through where it turns generic and what it leaves out. (What counts: your narration and its script. The genAI piece does not.)
  • Joining the conversation. Use Gemini Notebook's interactive Audio Overview to speak into the discussion; record the moment you intervene. (What counts: your own spoken part. The AI hosts do not.)
  • Marginalia. Answer a genAI text in the margins, line by line — an argument a reader can follow. (What counts: your margin notes. The genAI text does not.)

2 · Remix & Recombine

Bring outputs from more than one tool together into something neither made alone.

  • Compose with a text-to-music model. Draft and revise lyrics with a language model (Claude, Gemini), then perform and record the music and vocals yourself (your performance is what counts). Or write the lyrics yourself and have Suno or Udio turn them into a song (your lyrics are what count).
  • Avatar collage. Use an image generator (Midjourney, DALL-E) to illustrate people and places related to your topic. Print and cut them out. Use a glue stick, markers and tape to arrange your cutouts into one composed scene. (This counts as your transformed AI artifact.)
  • Sampled audio. Cut an excerpt of a genAI podcast into your own edited piece — reorder it, interrupt it, interleave your own lines. (What counts: your own lines and edits. Keep the podcast excerpt under about a tenth of the running time.)
  • Assembled video. Combine a generated song, generated images, and your own footage into a short edited video. (What counts: your own footage. The generated song and images do not.)
  • Staged debate. Prompt two AI personas into disagreement, then frame and cut their exchange so your reading comes through. (What counts: the frame you write around it. The AI exchange does not.)

3 · Deconstruct & Expose

Make the machine's limits and habits visible as your subject.

  • Side-by-side. Place the AI's generic passage next to the specific version you wrote from your primary source. (What counts: the version you wrote. The AI passage does not.)
  • Regression sequence. Pass one detail back through the model five or ten times, showing how it drifts toward the average. (What counts: what you write about the drift. The model's versions do not.)
  • Annotated transcript. Mark up a genAI output for every invention or empty generality, footnoted against your real sources. (What counts: your notes and footnotes. The genAI output does not.)
  • Marked-up draft. Have a model write a passage in Google Docs, then revise it in suggesting mode with margin comments. (What counts: your revisions and comments. The model's passage does not.)
  • What the machine could not know. Build a piece entirely from what your interview or artifact holds and the model got wrong. (What counts: the whole piece. It is all yours.)

4 · Recast the Modality

Take something the machine made in one form and remake it in another.

  • Timeline to illustration. Turn a Gemini Notebook timeline into a hand-drawn one. (What counts: your hand-drawn timeline.)
  • Briefing to performance. Turn an AI briefing into a spoken-word poem you perform. (What counts: your poem and your performance. The briefing does not.)
  • Data to song. Turn a research finding into a ballad or protest song. (What counts: your song.)
  • Dialogue to script. Turn genAI dialogue into a short skit you cast and act. (What counts: the script you write and the skit you act. The genAI dialogue does not.)

5 · Persona & Voice

Have the machine speak as someone, then complicate the impersonation.

  • Grounded persona interview. Load a model with a real corpus about a figure — a memoir, interviews, primary documents — and interview them. With the sources the answers gain specifics; without them you get generic mush. Then set the answers beside the person's actual words and mark where it invents. (What counts: your questions and your marking of what it invents. The AI's answers do not.)
  • Synthetic panel. Generate a panel of 'experts,' then fact-check their claims against your peer-reviewed source. (What counts: your fact-check. The panel does not.)
  • Synthetic narration. Use a voice tool (such as ElevenLabs) to narrate an archival passage — with a note on what a synthetic voice adds and costs. (What counts: your note on what the voice adds and costs. The synthetic voice does not.)

Ethics note — grounding a persona on a private or deceased person. A public figure with a published record is one thing; a private person — a late grandmother, a family member — is another. Treat the work as synthesizing a memory-corpus (letters, recordings, the family's recollections) and marking its limits, not as resurrecting a voice. Ask the family first. And consider whether the more honest piece is the one that shows what the machine could not recover.

6 · Anchor with the Real

The counterweights — no AI at all.

  • Your recorded real interview. Unedited — the primary source in its own voice. (What counts: the questions you prepared ahead of time. If the conversation was unplanned, your takeaways or your analysis of the interview count.)
  • Your own photographs, field footage, or a scanned artifact. (What counts: all of it. It is yours.)
  • A handwritten or hand-made piece, scanned in. (What counts: all of it. It is yours.)
  • Your raw field notes. (What counts: all of it. It is yours.)

Worked examples — good AI moves by topic

Every topic is built from the same seven facets — the who / what / when / where / why of it. Here they are filled for three very different subjects:

A public figure — Kendrick Lamar.

Good AI moves: grounded persona interview (his voice is specific enough that the generic version collapses fast); side-by-side ('write a verse like Kendrick' vs. a real verse); data or briefing to performance. Generic vs. specific: the model gives 'acclaimed, socially conscious rapper'; the specific is one bar, one sample, the thing the superfan told you.

A private person — your grandmother.

Good AI moves: anchor with the real (the unedited interview with your parent); what the machine could not know; persona and voice with the ethics note. Generic vs. specific: the encyclopedic decade she lived in vs. the particular her obituary and recipe card carry.

An idea/practice — veganism.

Good AI moves: staged debate (an ethicist against a rancher, fact-checked against your peer-reviewed source); data to song; marked-up draft (revise a model's 'why go vegan' toward honesty). Generic vs. specific: the machine gives the both-sides average; your one real interview and one real dataset give the stakes.
