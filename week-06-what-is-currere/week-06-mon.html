Week 6 · Monday — The Currere Begins

Act II · What is currere · one thread, across your whole life in schools
Today's goals: Learn the four moments, then find them in a poem. Close Act I with your own pages on the screen. Do the gushes — go back, go forward, look across — and find the one thread that runs through your life in schools.

0 · The four moments (~5 min)
  • From the Moore reading over the weekend. Four words, on the board:
  • Regressive — go back and gather. Memories in a stream, no order, no choosing.
  • Progressive — go forward and gather again. Write the future as if remembering it: what's likely, built out of the past you just gathered. Moore's word is "memories," in quotation marks. He adds that people find this one hard.
  • Analytical — hold the past gush and the future gush up against each other and name the themes and connections running through both. What runs through both is what's working on you now — Moore's "in-between space," and the thing you're really examining. Not what it means yet. What recurs.
  • Synthetical — hold three things at once (where you've been, where you're going, and those themes) and write what it changes about the teacher you intend to be. In this course that writing is the currere.
  • The one thing to hold onto: these are four kinds of attention you pay while you gather, not four sections of an essay. Currere is a thread across past, present, and future — not one dramatic moment, and not four labeled boxes.
    • Moore's own directions, word for word
    StageWhat Moore tells his students to do
    1 · Regression"Think back as far as you can and record your memories in a stream-of-consciousness manner. Consider teachers, experiences with education, impressions, and media — anything at all that is vivid to you as you grew up with the concepts of education and teaching."
    2 · Progression"Next, project yourself into your future and record 'memories' of things that are likely to happen based on this foundation of memories and ideas about teaching. (It is common to find this challenging.)"
    3 · Analysis"Look at the connections between your past memories and your ideas about the future. Make an objective identification of the common themes and connections between the first two stages."
    4 · Synthesis"Finally, pull the whole thing together — (where you've been, where you're going, and the common themes) — and write about how you will use this new self-knowledge to make conscious, informed decisions about your practice as an educator."

    Moore adapted these from Pinar for his MAT interns, and assigned the whole thing as an hour's work. Note stage 2: not "imagine," but likely, and built on the foundation of stage 1.

  • Act I taught you the regressive. Five weeks of learning to put a reader inside a real moment — OP4 on the senses alone, OP3 on the voices alone. In a published currere that is most of the page count.
  • What's new is the rest of it: stepping back from the moment to say what it means, and reaching forward into a future you haven't lived. OP3 and OP4 told you not to step back — the scene had to carry it by itself. Now you add what those assignments withheld, and you sustain it on one thread that matters.

Purpose: name the four before the poem, so the poem is something to practice on rather than a warm-up.

1 · Daily Poem — "Ethics," a currere in 25 lines (~15 min)
  • Linda Pastan, "Ethics" — read it aloud, then hear Pastan read it herself. An ethics teacher asked the same question every fall: in a museum fire, save the Rembrandt or the old woman? Years later the speaker stands in a real museum, before a real Rembrandt, an old woman herself.
  • Now hunt, in pairs, with the poem in front of you. Where is the regressive? The analytical? The synthetical? The progressive? Mark the lines. Don't settle for "it's in there somewhere" — point at words.
  • Round the room (~4 min). One moment per pair, with the line you're pointing at. Where did pairs disagree?
    • Where they are — after the round
    • Regressive: the ethics class itself. The hard chairs, the question asked "every fall," and the teacher's report — "Linda… eschews / the burdens of responsibility."
    • Analytical: "I know now that woman / and painting and season are almost one." Then-self and now-self in one sentence.
    • Synthetical: the last line — "and all beyond saving by children."
    • Progressive: it isn't there. The poem goes straight from the classroom to the present. The future she was asked to imagine — an old woman without many years left — has simply arrived: "old woman, / or nearly so, myself."
  • So: is this a currere? It was good enough for Pastan, and for the anthologies. Hold that question — you'll need it tonight.
  • The one thing to carry into the homework: "it isn't there" is a real finding. You just watched a published poem come up short by one moment. So when you go looking on your own and a moment won't come, write down that it isn't there. Don't manufacture one to fill the slot.

Purpose: twenty-five lines is small enough to mark in one sitting, and the missing progressive is the lesson — the four are attention, not architecture.

3 · The gushes, in Journaler (~20 min)

Open the Currere tab. Each moment is its own gush. The timer starts at 8:00, which is what you want for the first two — set it with − and ＋ if you need to. No stopping, no fixing. Gather the material; don't write "the essay" yet.

  • Regressive — go back. 8 minutes. Free-associate your life in schools, as far back as you can reach. Teachers, a classroom, a grade on a page, a thing said to you. No order. When the buzzer sounds, answer the question Romano asks about how the remembering went — that answer saves with the gush.
  • Progressive — go forward. 8 minutes. Don't invent a future from nothing — build it out of the gush you just wrote. What is likely to happen to you as a teacher, given all that? Write it as if you were remembering it. This is the one the published writers skip. It will feel harder than the first; Moore says so too. Let it be hard.
  • Analytical — past and future, side by side. 4 minutes, no timer. Your two gushes come up in facing panes, labelled past and future. Name the themes and connections running through both, in the box underneath. Whatever shows up in both is operating on you right now — that's the point of putting them next to each other. Not what it means — what recurs.
  • Synthetical — later. Putting it back together is the work of the coming weeks, not today.

Purpose: generate raw material for all four moments in one sitting, so the future gets written before the vivid past takes over. Eight minutes is long enough to get somewhere and short enough that nobody starts editing.

4 · From moments to a thread (~20 min)

In Journaler: Freewrite/OPs → Open page. This is where you named your first thread last Wednesday.

  • Pull five or six key moments from your life as a teacher and a learner. Concrete scenes, the kind that stay with you. One line each is enough.
  • Under each moment, write a possible theme, and how that moment has shaped you up to the present.
  • Read down your list. Which theme keeps coming back? Name it with ＋ New thread… and put the chip on this page. That recurring theme, running from those moments into who you are becoming, is your thread.
  • Your chips from last Wednesday are sitting right there. Same thread? Then you have it. Different? Then you have two candidates for the topic conference — that's fine too. Keep the page.

Purpose: start with moments, because that is where real memory lives, then let the thread surface from them. The chips make the recurrence visible, and the kept page is Currere work in the notebook.

5 · Launch the currere (~10 min)
  • Meet the Currere Guidelines — the self-study you build from here. The arc is topic conference → draft → draft conference → final draft + audio; each piece has its own one-page guideline (see the assessments overview for points and due dates). Final draft is due Friday, October 30.
  • Two shapes, both from pieces you've read: an open currere breaks into named parts that walk the four moments (J. Edwards, "Beyond Presence"); a braided one runs as one piece and holds its meaning until late (M. T. Edwards, "Who's Cheating Whom?"). Neither is right. Let your thread decide.
  • Heads-up: the first conference is a topic conference, next Monday — you'll bring a short packet (a couple of candidate threads, a currere whose form you admire, and Todd-in-a-Can's read of your threads).
Wrap-up & before Wednesday:
  • You have been reading currere since the first week of class — one piece a week, Weeks 1 through 5. We read them for other things at the time.
  • Pick one and find the four moments in it yourself, first. Write down what you see before you look at anything else.
  • If a moment won't come, don't force it — move to the next piece on the list and try again there. Keep going until you have found all four somewhere. That may take two pieces. It may take three.
  • Then check yourself against my read of all five. Bring a half sheet: one moment where we agree, one where we don't, and which of the five shapes yours will be closer to.
  • There's a Currere Tester on that sheet too — a prompt you paste into whatever AI you use. It may help. It is not a perfect tool, so use it with caution, and argue with it.
