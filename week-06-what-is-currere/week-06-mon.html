Week 6 · Monday — The Currere Begins

Act II · What is currere · one thread, across your whole life in schools
Today's goals: Learn the four moments, then find them in a poem. Close Act I with your own pages on the screen. Do the gushes — go back, go forward, look across — and find the one thread that runs through your life in schools.

0 · The four moments (~5 min)

  • From the Moore reading over the weekend. Four words, on the board:
  • Regressive — go back. Progressive — go forward. Analytical — lay them side by side. Synthetical — put it back together.
  • The one thing to hold onto: these are four kinds of attention you pay while you gather, not four sections of an essay. Currere is a thread across past, present, and future — not one dramatic moment, and not four labeled boxes.
  • Every One-Pager you wrote was a tiny currere — a scene plus a coda reaching across time. This is the full version, sustained on one thread that matters.

Purpose: name the four before the poem, so the poem is something to practice on rather than a warm-up.

1 · Daily Poem — "Ethics," a currere in 25 lines (~15 min)

  • Linda Pastan, "Ethics" — read it aloud, then hear Pastan read it herself. An ethics teacher asked the same question every fall: in a museum fire, save the Rembrandt or the old woman? Years later the speaker stands in a real museum, before a real Rembrandt, an old woman herself.
  • Now hunt. Where is the regressive? (The class. The hard chairs. "Linda… eschews the burdens of responsibility.") Where is the analytical? ("I know now that woman / and painting and season are almost one.") Where is the synthetical? (The last line.)
  • Where is the progressive? Take the time to look. It isn't there — the poem goes straight from the classroom to the present. The future she was asked to imagine, an old woman with not many years left, has simply arrived: "old woman, / or nearly so, myself."
  • So: is this a currere? It was good enough for Pastan. Hold the question — you'll see the same gap in four of the five published pieces you've already read.

Purpose: twenty-five lines is small enough to mark in one sitting, and the missing progressive is the lesson — the four are attention, not architecture.

2 · Housekeeping + Breaking the Rules on the screen (~10 min)

  • Five One-Pagers, from "Trust the Gush" to a page that broke the rules on purpose. Act I is closed.
  • Passages from your OP5s on the screen, no names. For each one: which rule is being broken, and what does breaking it buy the sentence?

Purpose: Act I ends with their own pages, read for craft, the way OP4 ended last week.

3 · The gushes, in Journaler (~20 min)

Open the Currere tab. Each moment is its own gush. The timer starts at 8:00, which is what you want for the first two — set it with − and ＋ if you need to. No stopping, no fixing. Gather the material; don't write "the essay" yet.

  • Regressive — go back. 8 minutes. Free-associate your life in schools, as far back as you can reach. Teachers, a classroom, a grade on a page, a thing said to you. No order. When the buzzer sounds, answer the question Romano asks about how the remembering went — that answer saves with the gush.
  • Progressive — go forward. 8 minutes. Imagine yourself teaching a year from now, five years from now. Free-associate that future the same way. This is the one the published writers skip. Don't skip it. It will feel harder than the first — let it be hard.
  • Analytical — lay them side by side. 4 minutes, no timer. Your two gushes come up in facing panes. What runs through both? Name it in the box underneath. Don't decide yet what it means.
  • Synthetical — later. Putting it back together is the work of the coming weeks, not today.

Purpose: generate raw material for all four moments in one sitting, so the future gets written before the vivid past takes over. Eight minutes is long enough to get somewhere and short enough that nobody starts editing.

4 · From moments to a thread (~20 min)

In Journaler: Freewrite/OPs → Open page. This is where you named your first thread last Wednesday.

  • Pull five or six key moments from your life as a teacher and a learner. Concrete scenes, the kind that stay with you. One line each is enough.
  • Under each moment, write a possible theme, and how that moment has shaped you up to the present.
  • Read down your list. Which theme keeps coming back? Name it with ＋ New thread… and put the chip on this page. That recurring theme, running from those moments into who you are becoming, is your thread.
  • Your chips from last Wednesday are sitting right there. Same thread? Then you have it. Different? Then you have two candidates for the topic conference — that's fine too. Keep the page.

Purpose: start with moments, because that is where real memory lives, then let the thread surface from them. The chips make the recurrence visible, and the kept page is Currere work in the notebook.

5 · Launch the currere (~10 min)

  • Meet the Currere Guidelines — the self-study you build from here. The arc is topic conference → draft → draft conference → final draft + audio; each piece has its own one-page guideline (see the assessments overview for points and due dates). Final draft is due Friday, October 30.
  • Two shapes, both from pieces you've read: an open currere breaks into named parts that walk the four moments (J. Edwards, "Beyond Presence"); a braided one runs as one piece and holds its meaning until late (M. T. Edwards, "Who's Cheating Whom?"). Neither is right. Let your thread decide.
  • Heads-up: the first conference is a topic conference, next Monday — you'll bring a short packet (a couple of candidate threads, a currere whose form you admire, and Todd-in-a-Can's read of your threads).
