The Four Moments, in Five Currere You Have Already Read

Week 6 · TCE 284 · every one of these is published in the Currere Exchange Journal, and all five are on Canvas

You have been reading currere since the first week of class. One piece a week, Weeks 1 through 5 — we read them for other things at the time, and mostly left the four moments alone.

Do this in order. Pick one of the five. Read it and write down the four moments as you find them yourself. Only then turn to my read of it, further down. If you cannot find one of the four, do not force it — go to the next piece on the list and try again there.

One thing to know going in, because it will save you from forcing an answer: the four moments are four kinds of attention a writer pays while gathering — not four sections, not an outline, and not a checklist a reader runs down. They are often out of order, one paragraph can do two at once, and a published currere can be missing one entirely. So if you cannot find one, the honest answer may be that it is not there.

The four moments, and how to spot them on a page

Regressive — going back

A scene. A particular room, a particular year, a teacher with a name. Past tense, close in, slowed down until you can see it.

Cues: a grade, a date, an object, a remembered voice in quotation marks

Progressive — going forward

A future imagined rather than predicted. Usually the shortest and shakiest part, and the one most often missing. Watch the tense shift forward.

Cues: I envision · I imagine · I want my students to · would, will, hope to, fear that

Analytical — laying them side by side

The writer steps out of the story and asks what it means. The camera pulls back. Often where a scholar gets cited. It can also be done entirely by image, with no explaining at all.

Cues: Looking back now · What I did not see then · a then-self and a now-self in the same sentence

Synthetical — putting it back together

The close. Past, present, and future held in one hand, and a so what about the writer’s own life — not a moral for everyone else.

Cues: the title coming back · an opening image returning changed · a present-tense sentence about who the writer is now

STOP. Write your own read first. Everything below is my answer key.

My read of the five

Before the table — the one pattern worth naming. Of these five published pieces, only one contains a passage where the writer imagines a future. Four never look ahead in the writer’s own voice at all, and two have no future-tense sentence anywhere in them. If the progressive was the moment you could not find, that is why, and you were reading correctly.

WK 1Who’s Cheating Whom? Academic Dishonesty as a Vehicle for Deep Learning · M. T. Edwards · CEJ 6(2), 2022

Shape: braided. Seven roman-numeral sections; five and a half of them are one continuous story.

REGRESSIVE§I–VIMiddvale High, 1987, two weeks to graduation. The beanbag chair, the blue-haired Miss Shupe, the dog treat pressed to Ellie Faukner’s lips, the exam left facedown on the copier glass, the waterbed and the Maxwell House, the blue books, the hand on his shoulder.
PROGRESSIVEnot foundNo imagined future. Search the piece for a future-tense sentence in his own voice and there is none — the only I will in it belongs to Miss Shupe, inside a scene. What leans forward instead is a present practice he keeps (I’ve shied away from timed, in-class exams) and a future belonging to everyone rather than to him (Alzheimer’s cases projected to more than double by 2050).
ANALYTICALinside §II, then §VIIBuried mid-scene: my relationship with her has continued to evolve … When I was 18, Miss Shupe represented everything I resented about school. However, as I’ve experienced the struggles of aging firsthand… Then §VII does it at length — including the sentence where the writing changes his mind: Until I wrote this currere reflection, I was convinced that I had cheated.
SYNTHETICALfinal ¶Miss Shupe’s tragic flaw wasn’t her anger, it was her genetics. The 18-year-old wrote her up as a tragic hero with anger as the fatal flaw. Thirty-five years later he re-runs Aristotle on the same subject and reassigns the hamartia.

What this one teaches you: the analytical can hide in the middle of a scene, and a currere can answer its own title sideways. Also — the essay he cheated with and the essay you are reading are the same essay with a different verdict.

WK 1–2How to Read a Blank Page of Writing · J. D. Wiederhold · CEJ 8(1), 2024

Shape: open, with named sections — but three of them are regressive, not one each.

REGRESSIVEthree sectionsA First Grade Blank Page — the memorized flashcard address, Mrs. Grenager pacing like a sentinel, the journal his dying mother wrote him. A Homeless Blank Page — the Greyhound to Provo at 16, drafting his own power-of-attorney filing on a Brother word processor, a year on the street, Mr. Baldwin’s AP Literature. Blank Page Baldwin Rage — and this one is from his own teaching: Dieme, tapping his temple, I’m thinking.
PROGRESSIVEnot foundNo imagined future. His last section is How I Now Read and React — present, not future. The forward-looking sentences in the piece are about his argument (existing strategies will be more effective), not about his own life ahead. The closest personal one is a past-tense wish: I wanted to not be the kind of teacher who…
ANALYTICALmid-scene + last sectionThe contradiction named as a question, right in the middle of the story: how had he drafted legal filings all while believing myself to be an incapable writer? Then the last section reads his own teaching against Gee and Freire.
SYNTHETICALlast lineHe handed in so many blank pages, and I didn’t read a single one. The title turns on the writer himself.

What this one teaches you: a regressive scene can come from your teaching, not only your schooling. And the strongest close can be an admission rather than a lesson.

WK 2–3“None of Us:” Four Poems About Dad · T. Daspit · CEJ 5(1), 2021

Shape: four dated poems — 7-23-17, 12.10.17, 6-19-2020, 11.7.2020. No prose at all.

REGRESSIVEall four poemsThe ’55 black Dodge with the stick on the column. His father fumbling for words at lunch, asking where am i? The flashlight collection, the stroke, the funeral, the Hank Williams movie. Room 427, and Ruth hiding her cigarettes in his dad’s room.
PROGRESSIVEenacted, not imaginedNo imagined future — and not one future-tense construction anywhere in the four poems. Instead the four dates are the time axis: the piece takes three and a half years to happen rather than looking ahead. Where a future does arrive it is dreaded in the past tense: everyone in the car knew there weren’t going to be too many more of these lunches. Decide for yourself whether that counts.
ANALYTICALby imageNo looking back now anywhere. The analytical is a gesture: in the dream his father stops the truck, turned to me / said / ‘your turn.’ And the lexicon passage — the machinists and carpenters he cannot speak like, against a different lexicon / one where gravity curls.
SYNTHETICALlast linesno dad, you won’t disappear / none of us in this hospital will. / none of us. The title arrives, and widens from one man to everyone.

What this one teaches you: a currere does not have to be an essay, and the analytical does not have to explain. Watch the I slide into you in the last poem — that is a Grammar B move, and you did it in Week 5.

WK 3–4Beyond Presence: The Importance of Being Seen · J. F. Edwards · CEJ 5(1), 2021

Shape: open — and the one piece in this set whose sections are the four moments, in order. Her headings even name them.

REGRESSIVEInvisibilitySophomore year, the yellow envelope, a boldfaced F in World History. Mr. Kraft over the black rims of his glasses: Where did you get this? He accuses her of cheating. The reveal: after nine weeks he did not know her name, and had been recording another girl’s grades in the row marked Jennifer.
PROGRESSIVEVisionThe only imagined future in all five pieces. Study this one. The verbs do it out loud: I envision them becoming an integral part of the campus culture · I envision a campus in which international students are recognized as assets · I imagine communities of diverse learners collaborating.
ANALYTICALInsightAs I revisit my 16-year-old self who had been accused of cheating by a history teacher who did not know her name… Then the two lives set side by side in one sentence: My experience with Mr. Kraft contributed as much to my pedagogy as any of the teacher preparation courses I took in college.
SYNTHETICALPerspectiveShe names what currere did — and then refuses a tidy ending: as I look around the college campus, I realize how great the distance is between my vision and the current situation. The close is a commitment, not a resolution.

What this one teaches you: this is the open shape, and the cleanest model if you want the four moments visible. Read it beside Who’s Cheating Whom? — both pieces turn on a teacher and an accusation of cheating, and they are built in opposite ways.

WK 4–5Moviehouse Poems (3) · M. O’Hara · CEJ 9(1), 2025

Shape: eight poems, no sections, no argument. The hardest of the five to map — on purpose.

REGRESSIVEdiffuseMostly a remembered habit rather than a dated scene — we slouch, the crowd ducks under the marquee. One sharp exception: From a revival of House of Wax one night / at the Sam Eric, what survives / is not the gimmicks of 3D but the fright / of Vincent Price’s face crumbling.
PROGRESSIVEnot foundAbsent, completely. Not one future-tense construction in eight poems. No future arrives, none is imagined, none is dreaded. It was published anyway.
ANALYTICALA Course in Being a SpectatorA whole poem doing the analytical work, and the title announces it: Watching is not a function of sight / as much as a feeling of mind— / an endeavor to take the touch of light / and twist it into meaning. Elsewhere it shrinks to one parenthesis: (Deceit has always been applauded.)
SYNTHETICALlast poemSoft, and only a few words wide: we depart the theater / wondering if any ending can be happy.

What this one teaches you: the moments can be the size of a parenthesis, and a whole piece can live without one of them. If you can find what is missing here, you can find what is missing in your own draft.

Is it still currere, if a moment is missing?

Worth settling with two sources rather than an opinion. Both are ones you can go check.

Moore, “Starting the Conversation” — the reading you did for Monday — never describes four parts of an essay. He calls currere an autobiographical method, the currere process, and the four-step currere process. His own directions to his students are headed Stage 1 – Regression, Stage 2 – Progression, Stage 3 – Analysis, Stage 4 – Synthesis. Stage 4 reads: pull the whole thing together … and write about how you will use this new self-knowledge. The writing comes after the four stages. He gives his students an hour.

The journal’s own call for manuscripts asks for work that explore[s] the Currere process through the use of autobiographical and narrative approaches, and adds: We also welcome short creative pieces, exploratory pieces, experimental pieces, poetry, etc. It asks for no four moments, no sections, and nothing about all four being present.

So the four moments are things the writer does. What you read is the trace of someone having done them. A writer can work through the progressive in a notebook, let it change what they notice, and never put a future paragraph on the page. None of these five is less currere for what it leaves out.

But the absence is not free

Which is not to say it does not matter. Read O’Hara again — eight poems, beautifully made, about having watched. And they go nowhere. Nothing in them is becoming anything. The progressive is the moment that keeps a currere from being a good memory with a lesson attached.

That is why your guidelines ask for it and Pastan’s did not. Those five writers were making an artifact; you are learning a method. The progressive is the stage people skip — four of these five skipped it — so it is the one you practice on purpose.

After you have written your own read

Bring a half sheet with two things:

1. One moment where my read and yours agree, and one where they do not — a moment I point to that you could not see even at the lines I give, or one you found that I missed. If you had to move to a second piece to find a moment, say which moment sent you there.

2. Which of the five shapes yours is going to be closer to, and one sentence on why.

My read is one reader’s, made years after the fact. None of these writers labeled their own moments, and some of them were not thinking in these terms while drafting.

The Currere Tester

It may help. It is not a perfect tool — use it with caution. Paste the prompt below into whatever AI you use, then paste in the currere you are reading. It points at lines; it does not grade, and it is wrong often enough that you should argue with it.

You are a Currere Tester. A student is learning to find the four moments of currere in a published piece of writing. You will be given the text of one piece. The four moments are four kinds of attention a writer pays while gathering material. They are NOT four sections, they are rarely in order, one paragraph can do two at once, and a published currere can be missing one entirely. Regressive — going back. A scene from the writer’s own past, rendered close enough to see. Progressive — going forward. A future the writer imagines rather than predicts. Analytical — laying them side by side. The writer steps out of the story to ask what it means. This can be done entirely by image, with no explaining. Synthetical — putting it back together. A close that holds past, present and future, with a “so what” about the writer’s own life. For each of the four, do one of two things. Either quote the actual lines you believe are doing that work and say in one sentence what makes them that kind of attention — or say plainly that you cannot find it in this piece. Never guess, never pad, and never invent a passage. Rules you do not break. You do not score the piece. You do not say whether it “is” or “is not” a currere, and you do not say whether it is good. You do not suggest what the writer should have added. If a moment is missing, that is a fact about the piece, not a flaw to fix. Name which moment you are least confident about and say why.
