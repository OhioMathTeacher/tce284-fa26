Week 6 · Wednesday — Currere in the Wild

Act II · What is currere · find your one thread
Today's goals: Compare what you found in five real curreres, see the journal you could publish in, and commit to the one thread you'll follow — the one that reaches back, sits in the present, and leans into your future.

0 · Daily Poem — "Tell all the truth but tell it slant" (~10 min)
  • Emily Dickinson, "Tell all the truth but tell it slant". Read it aloud. What does she mean by telling the truth "slant" instead of straight out? When have you told something true by coming at it sideways?

Purpose: a currere tells a true thing slant — through scene, not statement. The poem sets that up before we ask them to do it.

1 · What you found, and what you couldn't (~25 min)
  • Hands up (~2 min). Who found all four in the first piece? Who had to move to a second? A third?
  • Group up (~1 min). One corner per piece, by whichever of the five you worked on. Half sheets out.
  • Round one — what you both found (~8 min). Read out the lines where your read and mine agree, and say what makes them that kind of attention.
  • Round two — where we part (~10 min). Where did we disagree, or where could you find nothing at all? Bring it to the room and make me defend my answer. This is the round that matters.
  • Nobody read these looking for the moments the first time through. If a moment was hard to find, that is a fact about the piece, not about you.
  • Across the five: only "Beyond Presence" is built as four sections that match the four moments, and it is the only one in which the writer imagines a future. Four never look ahead in the writer's own voice — Daspit and O'Hara contain no future-tense sentence at all — and neither does Pastan's poem. Every one was published.
  • So is a piece missing a moment still a currere? Settle it from the sources, not from me. Moore never describes four parts of an essay — he says "method," "process," "four-step," and his own directions are headed Stage 1 – Regression through Stage 4 – Synthesis, with the writing coming after the four stages. The journal's call asks for work that "explore[s] the Currere process" and welcomes "poetry" and "experimental pieces." The moments are what the writer does; the essay is the trace.
  • The harder question (~4 min): what does a piece lose without it? O'Hara's last poem, read aloud. Eight poems about having watched, and nothing in them is becoming anything. Sit with that before answering.
  • Which is why your guidelines ask you for the progressive: you are learning the method, not publishing an artifact — and it is the stage four of these five writers skipped.
  • Two minutes on the Currere Exchange Journal itself — run out of Miami, and the current issue is twenty-two pieces on scripted curriculum. Who writes here? What would it take for a piece of yours to belong?

Purpose: they have read these five, so checking their own read against mine is closer work than hunting a sixth piece — and the missing progressive shows up as a pattern rather than a rule.

2 · Walk the Guidelines (~10 min)
  • With a partner, read the Currere Guidelines and skim the assessments overview, then write down every question a careful person should be able to answer: When is it due? How many drafts? How long? What is it actually about? How is it graded?
  • Answers together (~5 min). Every question, out loud, as a class — including the two one-on-one conferences and the author's audio at the end.

3 · From moments to a thread (~25 min)

In Journaler: Freewrite/OPs → Open page. This is where you named your first thread two weeks ago.

  • Six moments (~8 min). Pull five or six key moments from your life as a teacher and a learner. Concrete scenes, the kind that stay with you. One line each is enough.
  • A theme under each (~7 min). Under every moment, write a possible theme, and how that moment has shaped you up to now.
  • Name the one that keeps coming back (~5 min). Read down your list. Which theme recurs? Name it with ＋ New thread… and put the chip on this page. That recurring theme, running from those moments into who you are becoming, is your thread.
  • Test it (~5 min). Set it beside Monday's two gushes, the piece you read last night, and the chips you made two weeks ago. Does it reach into your past, sit in your present, and lean toward the teacher you're becoming? If it only lives in the past, keep looking. In Notebook → Threads you can see every thread you've named and how many pages sit under each — the count isn't the answer, but it's evidence.
  • Same thread as two weeks ago? Then you have it. Different? Then you have two candidates for the topic conference — that's fine too. Keep the page.

Purpose: start with moments, because that is where real memory lives, then let the thread surface from them. This lands after the gushes and after they have watched a published writer carry a thread, which is the right order — and the kept page is Currere work in the notebook.
